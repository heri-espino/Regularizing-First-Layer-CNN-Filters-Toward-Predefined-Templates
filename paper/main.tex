\title{Activation-Patching Effects in Controlled CNNs Depend on Intervention Size, Network Architecture, and First-Layer Filter Structure}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Activation patching measures how a model output changes when internal activations are replaced, but the result depends on how the intervention is defined. We study this problem in controlled convolutional networks whose first layer is initialized from a predefined bank of edge, corner, and ring filters. Constant regularization keeps the first-layer filters close to the predefined bank, whereas annealing the penalty allows them to move away from it during training. On matched counterfactual image pairs, a pre-specified 20-block test on independent renderer blocks shows that the difference in patching effect between release and retention changes sharply with intervention size, defined here by the number $k$ of patched first-layer channels; the same pre-specified contrast between $k=1,2$ and $k=4,8$ remains positive under centered-logit fidelity and unnormalized probability-error reduction, although the pointwise curves differ. A separate 100-block study across 16 CNN architectures finds large differences across architectures under both evaluation metrics, and the same conclusion holds after Greenhouse--Geisser correction, permutation tests, Friedman tests, and random-channel analyses. Finally, a 100-block pixel-permuted control study on independent renderer blocks compares the original filter bank with a common pixel permutation that preserves the template-bank Gram matrix, rank, singular spectrum, row norms, and coefficient multisets. For channels selected on the validation set, the centered-logit difference averaged across architectures lies outside a pre-specified equivalence margin based on the earlier architecture study, whereas the 90\% interval for the probability-error difference lies within its corresponding margin despite excluding zero. The spatial effect reverses sign in one architecture and is substantially smaller on average for random channel sets. Filter similarity alone therefore does not determine the patching effect: the result also depends on intervention size, network architecture, evaluation metric, and the spatial arrangement of the first-layer filters.\footnote{ChatGPT (OpenAI) was used as an assistive tool during experimental planning, code development and debugging, manuscript organization, and language editing. All experimental protocols, analyses, interpretations, and manuscript text were reviewed by the authors, who take full responsibility for the work.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

The first convolutional layer is one of the few places in a vision network where learned weights can still be inspected directly as spatial filters. This has motivated a long line of work asking whether early spatial filters should be learned freely at all. Wavelet and Gabor constructions provide useful fixed image representations \citep{bruna_2013_scattering-networks}. Gabor initialization, maintaining Gabor filter structure during training, learnable Gabor filters, and predefined-filter networks introduce stronger first-layer inductive biases \citep{ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization,molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor,linse_2024_predefined-filters,orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. Fixed or even random spatial kernels can also remain effective when later layers learn how to recombine them \citep{gaudio_2023_fixed-filters,gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions}. Analyses of unconstrained CNNs likewise find regular first-layer frequency structure and sensitivity of early filters to training-data statistics \citep{chowers_2023_first-layer-filters,jorgenson26ajorgenson_2026_early-cnn-filters}. These results motivate structured first-layer filters, but they leave open a different question: if the first layer remains close to the predefined filters, does that similarity correspond to a consistent patching effect?

The distinction matters because representational alignment and effects on model behavior are not interchangeable. Network Dissection, TCAV, Concept Whitening, concept bottlenecks, and related approaches measure or impose different forms of alignment between internal representations and human-defined concepts \citep{bau_2017_network-dissection,kim_2018_tcav,chen_2020_concept-whitening,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks}. Subsequent work shows that apparently aligned representations can leak unintended information, fail to correspond to intended semantics, or dissociate from effects on model outputs \citep{margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,havasi_2022_concept-leakage,marconato_2022_glancenets,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. A filter can therefore remain similar to a predefined spatial filter without implying that interventions on the corresponding activation channels behave in a simple or stable way.

Activation patching provides such an intervention: replace an internal activation from one run with the corresponding activation from another and measure the downstream output change. Yet the measured patching effect is not invariant to the experimental setup. Its interpretation depends on patch direction and scope, component granularity, source and target inputs, and evaluation metric \citep{heimersheim_2024_activation-patching,zhang_2024_activation-patching}. More generally, circuit-faithfulness scores can change under reasonable changes in ablation methodology, so the evaluation procedure partly defines the question being asked \citep{miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. These concerns are well established in mechanistic-interpretability work, but much less is known about how they interact with explicitly structured first-layer filters in a controlled vision setting.

We use small CNNs and procedural image tasks to isolate this question. Every model begins with the same $16$-channel, $9\times9$ first convolution. One condition is kept near a predefined bank of edge, corner, and ring filters by a constant penalty; another begins at the same bank but is released by annealing that penalty to zero. Matched counterfactual image pairs let us replace complete first-layer feature maps using activations from another valid input rather than an artificially corrupted image. We ask three questions. Does the difference in patching effect between release and retention depend on intervention size? With the patching location fixed, does changing the network architecture after the first layer change that difference? Finally, is any such effect specific to the original two-dimensional arrangement of the predefined filters, rather than to regularization toward a fixed filter bank, rank, or other non-spatial properties?

We do not interpret structured filters as inherently interpretable. Instead, we test when preserving their structure changes the measured patching effect. Our main findings are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A pre-specified test on independent renderer blocks shows that the difference in patching effect between release and retention depends strongly on intervention size. The pre-specified contrast between $k=1,2$ and $k=4,8$ remains positive under centered-logit fidelity and unnormalized probability error reduction, although the detailed pointwise curves depend on the metric.
\item A separate pre-specified study across 16 CNN architectures shows large differences across architectures under both evaluation metrics. The same conclusion holds after Greenhouse--Geisser correction, within-block permutation tests, Friedman tests, and random channel subsets.
\item A pixel-permuted control experiment on independent renderer blocks isolates first-layer spatial filter structure. Relative to a common pixel permutation that preserves the original bank's complete Gram matrix, rank, singular spectrum, row norms, and coefficient multisets, the centered-logit difference for channels selected on the validation set lies outside the pre-specified equivalence margin. That effect is architecture-dependent, reverses sign in one architecture, and is substantially smaller for random channel sets.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these results show that filter similarity does not determine the patching effect, and that channel selection changes the observed effect.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Structured and predefined spatial filters.}
Hand-designed spatial filters predate modern CNNs, and fixed wavelet scattering shows that substantial invariance and discriminative structure can be obtained without learning arbitrary spatial kernels \citep{bruna_2013_scattering-networks}. More recent CNN work has used Gabor banks as initialization \citep{ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization}, explicitly maintained Gabor structure during optimization \citep{molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure}, constrained Gabor parameters in task-specific first layers \citep{wang_2024_learnable-gabor}, and learned texture branches built from Gabor filters \citep{zhu_2023_gabor-texture}. Orozco-Solis et al.\ show that learnable Gabor filters can lose their initial structure over training and use similarity measures to track that degeneration \citep{orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. At a broader architectural level, networks with predefined or fixed spatial filters can retain substantial predictive performance \citep{linse_2024_predefined-filters,gaudio_2023_fixed-filters}, while random frozen convolutions can be effective when learned linear combinations are sufficiently expressive \citep{gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions}. Analyses of ordinary CNNs further suggest that first-layer energy profiles are highly regular and approximately whitening \citep{chowers_2023_first-layer-filters}, and that early-filter updates reflect properties of the training distribution \citep{jorgenson26ajorgenson_2026_early-cnn-filters}. This literature establishes that first-layer spatial structure can be useful and persistent. Our question is whether preserving one such structure yields a consistent patching effect.

\paragraph{Alignment, concepts, and effects on model behavior.}
Concept-based interpretability methods quantify or impose semantic organization at different representational levels. Network Dissection measures unit-level alignment to visual concepts \citep{bau_2017_network-dissection}; TCAV tests directional sensitivity to user-defined concept vectors \citep{kim_2018_tcav}; Concept Whitening rotates latent axes toward specified concepts \citep{chen_2020_concept-whitening}; and concept bottleneck models expose an intermediate concept representation that can be edited at test time \citep{koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks}. These approaches also expose a recurring limitation: an apparently meaningful representation need not be pure, complete, or used in the intended way. Concept bottlenecks can fail semantic alignment or leak task information \citep{margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,havasi_2022_concept-leakage,marconato_2022_glancenets}; concept-vector explanations can vary across layers, become entangled, or depend on spatial position \citep{nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability}; and recent causal analyses distinguish information that is decodable from information that changes model behavior \citep{sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. We adopt the same distinction at a lower level: filter-template similarity is a structural property, not evidence by itself that interventions on the corresponding activation channels have a stable effect on model behavior.

\paragraph{Activation patching and sensitivity to methodological choices.}
Direct interventions on visual-network units predate current activation-patching practice: Bau et al.\ activate or suppress internal CNN/GAN units to test their effect on classification and generated visual content \citep{bau_2020_causal-units}. Activation patching, interchange interventions, and related causal-abstraction tools generalize the idea of intervening on internal states rather than merely decoding them \citep{geiger_2025_causal-abstraction}. Practical guidance emphasizes that patching direction, component granularity, input choice, and evaluation metric change what evidence an experiment provides \citep{heimersheim_2024_activation-patching}. Zhang and Nanda show that alternative corruption methods and metrics can yield disparate localization results and caution in particular about nonlinear probability metrics and out-of-distribution corruptions \citep{zhang_2024_activation-patching}. Miller et al.\ similarly show that circuit faithfulness depends strongly on ablation methodology and argue that the task assigned to a circuit cannot be separated from the evaluation procedure \citep{miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. Broader work questions whether mechanistic explanations are unique or identifiable without additional assumptions \citep{meloux_2025_mechanistic-identifiability,sutter_2025_causal-abstraction-limits}. Our contribution is therefore not the general observation that patching is sensitive to methodological choices. Prior work on structured filters primarily studies filter structure or predictive performance, while prior work on activation patching primarily studies intervention methodology. Here we patch the same first-layer activations, vary how long the predefined filter structure is maintained, measure how the result changes with intervention size and network architecture, and then compare the original two-dimensional arrangement with a control matched on Gram matrix, rank, and singular spectrum.

\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Controlled tasks and predefined first-layer filters}\label{sec:setup}

We use two $32\times32$ grayscale procedural classification tasks with matched counterfactual pairs. Each renderer block contains 512 training, 256 validation, and 256 test images. Rendering varies angle, line width, scale, translation, Gaussian noise, and occasional occlusion. In \texttt{single\_shape}, the four classes are line, circle, triangle, and square. In \texttt{two\_concepts}, circle and triangle presence define two binary factors and four classes; a counterfactual pair changes one factor while holding the other factor and nuisance variables fixed. Earlier experiments showed a clearer dependence on intervention size on \texttt{two\_concepts}; subsequent follow-ups on independent renderer blocks therefore designated \texttt{two\_concepts} as the primary task and retained \texttt{single\_shape} as a pre-specified secondary task. This task choice was based on the earlier results but specified before evaluation of each later follow-up.

Every model begins with a bias-free $1\!\to\!16$ convolution with $9\times9$ kernels, same padding, and ReLU. The predefined bank contains eight oriented edges, four two-ray corners, and four rings. Each template is centered and unit norm; the $16\times81$ flattened bank has numerical rank 10. \Cref{fig:overview} shows the renderer, the bank, and the intervention point.

For learned filter $W_i$ and template $T_j$, structural similarity is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:template-similarity}
A=\frac{1}{16}\sum_i\max_j\langle \widehat W_i,\widehat T_j\rangle,
\end{equation}
where the inner product is signed cosine similarity after centering and normalizing each filter. A one-to-one matching analysis is retained as a secondary structural check.

\subsection{Training conditions and filter-bank controls}\label{sec:training}

Template-based models initialize the first convolution from the predefined filter bank $W_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and optimize
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:training-objective}
\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}+
\lambda(e)\frac{\lVert W-W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2}
{\lVert W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2}.
\end{equation}
The central training comparison uses the same initialization but different regularization schedules. \emph{Retention} keeps $\lambda(e)=1$ for all 200 epochs. \emph{Release} keeps $\lambda=1$ through epoch 10, decays linearly to zero by epoch 80, and remains zero thereafter. Adam uses learning rate $0.003$ and batch size 128, with no learning-rate scheduler or early stopping. This is a contrast between two complete training regimes, not a post-training intervention that changes only the first-layer kernels while holding the remainder of the trained network fixed: downstream parameters co-adapt to the evolving first layer in each regime.

The pixel-permuted control experiment on independent renderer blocks replaces the original reference filter bank with three alternatives while preserving the same retention/release schedules. The primary control, \emph{pixel-permuted}, applies one common random permutation of the 81 spatial coordinates to every row of the structured bank. Because the same permutation is applied to all rows, it preserves the complete $16\times16$ Gram matrix, row norms, numerical rank, singular spectrum, and each row's coefficient multiset while destroying the original two-dimensional edge/corner/ring arrangement. Two secondary controls use a generic random rank-10 bank and a generic random full-rank bank. Random filter-bank realizations are paired across task, architecture, and treatment within each block/initialization replicate.

\paragraph{Network architecture variants.}
The architecture study keeps the first convolution and patching location fixed and varies only the network after the patched layer. Its 16 variants use global max or global average pooling and controlled convolutional depths of 1, 2, or 4. Additional contrasts vary downstream width (16 or 64 channels for plain networks), residual versus plain depth-4 connectivity, and the presence or absence of downstream BatchNorm. All BatchNorm layers occur after the patched first-layer tensor. The pixel-permuted control study uses a balanced four-architecture subset containing the original depth contrast and the pooling factor that showed a clear difference in the completed architecture study: \texttt{tiny\_gmp}, \texttt{tiny\_gap}, \texttt{plain2\_w16\_gmp}, and \texttt{plain2\_w16\_gap}. That four-architecture subset was chosen based on the earlier architecture results but specified before any pixel-permuted control outcome was inspected. Exact definitions and parameter counts are reported in \Cref{app:architecture-posthoc}.

\subsection{Activation patching, ranking, and evaluation metrics}\label{sec:patching}

For each matched base/counterfactual pair, we cache the post-ReLU first-layer tensor for both images. A patched run starts from the base image and replaces selected feature maps with the corresponding maps from the counterfactual run. No test-set quantity is used to select channels.

Channels are ranked by the absolute standardized validation-set difference in global-max first-layer activation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:channel-ranking}
d_{g,j}=
\frac{\overline z_{g=1,j}-\overline z_{g=0,j}}
{s_j+10^{-6}},
\end{equation}
where $z_j$ is the global maximum of feature map $j$, $s_j$ is its validation-set standard deviation, and $g$ is the factor changed by the counterfactual. For \texttt{two\_concepts}, $g$ is the binary circle or triangle factor that is toggled in that counterfactual; for \texttt{single\_shape}, it is the destination shape identity. Channels are ranked by $|d_{g,j}|$ separately for that concept/identity, and the ranking is fixed using validation data before test patching. The same global-max ranking rule is used for every architecture, including GAP variants, so ranking does not change together with architecture. Eight random channel orders of the same size are evaluated as a control for the validation-based ranking.

Let
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:probability-error}
E_{\mathrm{prob}}(a,b)=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert p_a^{(i)}-p_b^{(i)}\rVert_2^2
\end{equation}
denote mean squared probability-space error over $N$ matched pairs. The original patching score is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:probability-fidelity}
F_{\mathrm{prob}}(S)=
1-\frac{E_{\mathrm{prob}}(S,1)}{E_{\mathrm{prob}}(0,1)}.
\end{equation}
This score measures normalized reconstruction of the counterfactual output; it is not a mediated fraction or a general measure of causal importance.

Because the denominator in \cref{eq:probability-fidelity} varies across models and treatments, later analyses use two alternative evaluation metrics. Centered logits remove the arbitrary common-logit offset,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:centered-logits}
\widetilde{\ell}=\ell-\mathrm{mean}(\ell)\mathbf 1,
\end{equation}
and centered-logit fidelity applies the same normalized reconstruction form in that space. The unnormalized probability error reduction is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:probability-error-reduction}
R_{\mathrm{prob}}(S)=
E_{\mathrm{prob}}(0,1)-E_{\mathrm{prob}}(S,1).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Pre-specified contrast and study design}\label{sec:estimand}

For any patching metric $M$, define the within-block release-minus-retention difference at intervention size $k$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:treatment-difference}
\Delta_b^M(k)=M_b^{\mathrm{release}}(k)-M_b^{\mathrm{retention}}(k),
\end{equation}
and the pre-specified contrast between $k=1,2$ and $k=4,8$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:channel-count-contrast}
B_b^M=
\frac{\Delta_b^M(4)+\Delta_b^M(8)}{2}
-\frac{\Delta_b^M(1)+\Delta_b^M(2)}{2}.
\end{equation}
A positive $B$ means only that the release-minus-retention difference is larger on average at $k=4,8$ than at $k=1,2$. It does not imply monotonicity in $k$ or a positive release effect at either larger intervention size.

Matched image pairs are repeated measurements within a model/block and are never treated as independent replicates. The studies were conducted sequentially rather than specified as one joint preregistration. Earlier analyses motivated a pre-specified 20-block \texttt{two\_concepts} test of the effect of intervention size in TinyCNN. Its architecture contrast and the pre-specified alternative-metric reanalysis then motivated a separate 100-block architecture study with 16 architecture variants. The architecture results then motivated the pixel-permuted control and the balanced four-architecture subset used on a further 100 independent renderer blocks. Each follow-up protocol and its primary analyses were specified before data from its new renderer blocks were evaluated, although the design of each follow-up was informed by the preceding results.

The 16-architecture and pixel-permuted control studies each average four paired initialization or initialization/filter-bank replicates within renderer block before inference. Their inferential sample size is therefore $n=100$ renderer blocks, not the 25,600 trained models in each grid. The alternative-metric reanalysis of saved checkpoints and the later Greenhouse--Geisser, permutation, Friedman, and random-channel analyses are post-hoc with respect to the original outcomes, although their analysis definitions were specified before recomputation. Earlier and secondary schedule analyses are retained in the appendix but are not pooled with tests on independent renderer blocks.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Filter similarity does not determine the patching effect}\label{sec:structural}

Retention maintains filter similarity as intended. In the larger schedule/task sensitivity sample, \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN models under constant regularization have mean template similarity 0.944 versus 0.574 under release, while mean test accuracy is 98.05\% versus 99.45\%. In the corresponding two-layer model, similarities are 0.999 and 0.896 while accuracies are 99.11\% and 99.26\%. One-to-one matching gives the same conclusion about filter similarity and rules out the trivial explanation that many learned filters merely match the same few templates (\Cref{app:kernel-matching}).

\Cref{fig:kernel-gallery} provides a representative visualization. It is treated as evidence about filter similarity only: neither visual resemblance nor the score in \cref{eq:template-similarity} establishes semantic purity or an effect on model behavior.

\caption{Representative first-layer kernels from a \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN checkpoint. The top row shows the predefined bank; later rows show saved filters under the earlier training conditions, reordered by one-to-one template matching for display. The reported statistical results use replicate-level filter-similarity and patching measurements rather than this example.}

That difference in filter similarity does not determine the activation-patching effect. In the pre-specified 20-block TinyCNN test, the original probability-fidelity contrast is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prospective-channel-result}
\bar B_{\mathrm{prob}}=0.33098,
\qquad
95\%\ \mathrm{CI}=[0.27368,0.38827],
\end{equation}
with $t_{19}=12.09$, $p=2.28\times10^{-10}$, and all 20 block-level values positive. The pointwise release-minus-retention differences are $-0.3459$, $-0.3317$, $-0.0221$, and $+0.0065$ at $k=1,2,4,8$, respectively. Thus a single choice of $k$ would describe only one part of the release-minus-retention comparison.

\subsection{Intervention-size contrast across evaluation metrics}\label{sec:metric-robustness}

The original probability fidelity has a model-specific denominator, and the pre-specified metric reanalysis shows that this denominator changes substantially between retention and release. The denominator $E_{\mathrm{prob}}(0,1)$ differs between release and retention by $+0.8486$ on average in TinyCNN, while the corresponding centered-logit denominator differs by $+126.54$. The normalization therefore affects the comparison.

Nevertheless, the pre-specified contrast in \cref{eq:channel-count-contrast} remains positive under both alternative metrics. Centered-logit fidelity gives $B=+0.17888$ (95\% CI $[+0.12727,+0.23049]$, Holm $p=6.95\times10^{-7}$), while probability error reduction gives $B=+0.89441$ (95\% CI $[+0.78630,+1.00252]$, Holm $p=8.59\times10^{-13}$).

Panel~(a) of \cref{fig:main-results} shows that the alternative metrics support the same \emph{sign of the pre-specified $B$ contrast}, but not the same values at each $k$. In particular, unnormalized probability error reduction becomes strongly positive at $k=4,8$, whereas the original normalized probability score is close to zero there. The alternative metrics therefore support the same pre-specified contrast, but they are not interchangeable.

\subsection{Activation-patching results depend on network architecture}\label{sec:architecture}

With the first-layer patching procedure, training conditions, validation ranking, counterfactual pairs, and evaluation metrics fixed, changing only the architecture after the first layer produces large differences:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:architecture-omnibus-clogit}
F_{15,1485}=77.09,\quad \eta_p^2=0.438
\end{equation}
for centered-logit fidelity and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:architecture-omnibus-prob}
F_{15,1485}=656.70,\quad \eta_p^2=0.869
\end{equation}
for probability error reduction. On these independent blocks, the TinyGMP-minus-Plain2-GMP contrast is $+0.16226$ (95\% CI $[+0.14970,+0.17481]$) for centered logits and $+0.88960$ (95\% CI $[+0.86212,+0.91707]$) for probability error reduction.

The pattern in panel~(b) of \cref{fig:main-results} is not a single monotone effect of depth or pooling. Under GMP, most of the Tiny-to-plain shift occurs when the first downstream convolution is added; adding further plain depth changes little. Under GAP, the depth pattern differs. GAP-versus-GMP contrasts are large but reverse direction across backbones. Residual-versus-plain depth-4 contrasts are comparatively small, while BatchNorm effects depend on depth, pooling, and metric. These results show architecture dependence rather than a universal effect of any single architectural component.

Two post-hoc analyses, specified before recomputation, test whether the architecture result depends on sphericity assumptions or on the validation-based channel ranking. First, the architecture effect remains significant after Greenhouse--Geisser correction, 100,000 within-block permutations, and Friedman rank tests; the Holm-adjusted permutation $p$ value is $2.0\times10^{-5}$ for both evaluation metrics. Second, differences across architectures remain when $B$ is computed from the mean over eight random channel orders, again with Holm permutation $p=2.0\times10^{-5}$ for both metrics. The architecture result is therefore not solely produced by the validation-derived channel ranking. Ranking still matters quantitatively: Spearman correlation across architecture means for selected versus random channels is $0.256$ for centered logits and $0.724$ for probability error reduction (\Cref{app:architecture-posthoc}).

\subsection{Effect of the two-dimensional filter arrangement}\label{sec:anchor-specificity}

We next tested whether the effect depended on the original edge/corner/ring arrangement or arose from regularizing the first layer toward a fixed filter bank more generally. The pixel-permuted control experiment addresses this directly. Its primary control applies the same random permutation of spatial coordinates to every template, preserving the full Gram matrix, numerical rank, singular spectrum, row norms, and row coefficient multisets while destroying the original two-dimensional arrangement.

Averaged over the four architectures on \texttt{two\_concepts}, selected-channel centered-logit $B$ differs between the structured and pixel-permuted filter banks by $+0.05743$ (95\% CI $[+0.05116,+0.06369]$, Holm sign-flip $p=2.0\times10^{-5}$). Before these outcomes were inspected, the equivalence margin was pre-specified as 20\% of the mean absolute $B$ from the earlier architecture study for the same four architectures. The centered-logit 90\% interval $[+0.05219,+0.06266]$ lies entirely outside that pre-specified equivalence margin ($\pm0.01704$), so the effect averaged across architectures differs from zero and lies outside the pre-specified equivalence margin.

For probability error reduction, the difference averaged across architectures is $+0.04563$ (95\% CI $[+0.03728,+0.05397]$, Holm sign-flip $p=2.0\times10^{-5}$). Its 90\% interval $[+0.03864,+0.05261]$ lies inside the wider pre-specified equivalence margin ($\pm0.06594$). Thus the probability-error contrast averaged across architectures differs from zero, but its 90\% interval lies within the pre-specified equivalence margin. This equivalence statement depends on the margin defined from the earlier architecture study; it does not imply a negligible effect of spatial arrangement in every architecture.

The effect also varies strongly across architectures. For channels selected on the validation set, the difference between the structured and pixel-permuted filter banks is positive for \texttt{tiny\_gmp}, \texttt{tiny\_gap}, and \texttt{plain2\_w16\_gap}, but negative for \texttt{plain2\_w16\_gmp} under both evaluation metrics. The interaction has partial $\eta^2=0.481$ for centered logits and $0.614$ for probability error reduction, with Holm permutation $p=2.0\times10^{-5}$ for both. Panel~(a) of \cref{fig:anchor-specificity} shows the sign reversal directly.

For random channel subsets, the difference in $B$ between the structured and pixel-permuted banks, averaged across architectures, is $+0.00571$ for centered logits and $+0.02007$ for probability error reduction, and both effects fall within the pre-specified equivalence margins. These averages are substantially smaller than the corresponding centered-logit effect for channels selected on the validation set, although architecture-dependent random-channel differences remain detectable. The effect of the original spatial arrangement is therefore larger on average for channels selected on the validation set than for random subsets, although it is not absent for random subsets.

Finally, the secondary filter-bank controls argue against numerical rank as the main explanation. On the primary task, the pixel-permuted and generic random rank-10 filter banks are generally similar, and differences between the random rank-10 and random full-rank filter banks are generally small after the pre-specified family corrections. The original two-dimensional organization is the filter-bank property most clearly isolated by the selected-channel result, but its sign and practical magnitude remain architecture- and evaluation-metric-dependent.

\subsection{Checks and secondary analyses}\label{sec:secondary}

The pixel-permuted control experiment on independent renderer blocks completes all 25,600 planned training jobs and evaluations. Maximum full-patch and no-op logit identity errors are zero, and the maximum structured-versus-pixel-permuted Gram-matrix discrepancy is $6.66\times10^{-16}$. The earlier independent checkpoint audit also reproduces central metrics from the earlier analyses at 32 fixed checkpoints within pre-specified numerical tolerances. Secondary schedule, task, ranking, and energy-matched analyses are reported in the appendix as supporting analyses and are not treated as independent replications of the tests on independent renderer blocks.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Maintaining first-layer filter structure and measuring activation-patching effects answer different questions. Work on predefined, fixed, and Gabor-based filters shows that spatial structure can be useful, robust, or even unnecessary for predictive performance depending on how later layers recombine it \citep{linse_2024_predefined-filters,gaudio_2023_fixed-filters,gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions,molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure}. Our retention condition confirms that such structure can be maintained almost exactly. But high filter similarity does not determine a unique patching effect. The difference between retention and release patching effects changes with intervention size, and the same first-layer intervention propagates differently through different architectures.

Under centered-logit fidelity, selected-channel $B$ averaged across architectures differs clearly from the pixel-permuted control, which preserves the non-spatial properties of the filter bank. This is not a universal benefit of the original templates: the effect reverses in one architecture, the probability-error contrast averaged across architectures falls within the pre-specified equivalence margin, and the random-channel contrasts averaged across architectures are smaller. These results show that the spatial arrangement changes the release-versus-retention patching effect, with both magnitude and direction depending on architecture, evaluation metric, and channel selection.

The retention-versus-release contrast should also not be read as the isolated causal effect of first-layer geometry in an otherwise fixed trained network. The regularization schedule changes optimization throughout training, and downstream weights co-adapt to the evolving first layer. The comparison therefore concerns two complete training regimes and asks how their activation-patching effects differ.

This distinction parallels a broader lesson from concept-based and mechanistic interpretability. Alignment, decodability, and causal influence answer different questions \citep{margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. Likewise, activation patching is a family of interventions whose conclusions depend on intervention and evaluation choices \citep{heimersheim_2024_activation-patching,zhang_2024_activation-patching,miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. Our experiments show that these methodological choices also matter in a controlled vision setting with deliberately structured first-layer filters and exact counterfactual pairs. Changing intervention size, network architecture, evaluation metric, first-layer filter structure, or ranking rule changes the quantity being measured.

These results suggest reporting intervention size, the type of patched component, evaluation metric, channel-selection rule, network architecture, and source and target inputs alongside activation-patching results. Filter similarity should be reported separately from effects measured by activation patching. When a null comparison is scientifically meaningful, equivalence should be tested rather than inferred from nonsignificance. These practices do not make patching a complete mechanistic explanation, but they make the claim being tested substantially clearer.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item The experiments use two related synthetic image tasks, one family of first-layer spatial templates, and small CNNs. Exact counterfactual pairs provide tight experimental control, but the results do not establish natural-image, large-CNN, vision-transformer, or language-model generalization.
\item Patching replaces complete post-ReLU feature maps with activations from an in-distribution matched counterfactual. The source activations are therefore not generated by artificial input corruption, but the hybrid activation state can still be off-manifold for the trained model.
\item The validation ranking uses global-max first-layer activation for every architecture, including GAP variants. This keeps ranking fixed while architecture changes, but it may favor some representations. Random-channel analyses show that architecture dependence remains without that ranking, while the pixel-permuted control experiment on independent renderer blocks shows that the effect of spatial arrangement is much smaller for random channels.
\item The original normalized probability score has a model-specific denominator that differs across treatments. Later analyses therefore use centered-logit and unnormalized probability evaluation metrics. Agreement is strongest at the level of the pre-specified $B$ contrast and architecture omnibus; values at individual intervention sizes and conclusions about effect size can differ across metrics.
\item The probability-error difference between the structured and pixel-permuted banks, averaged across architectures, differs from zero but falls within the pre-specified equivalence margin, defined as 20\% of the mean absolute $B$ from the earlier architecture study. That margin was pre-specified before outcomes on the independent renderer blocks were inspected, but it is a study convention tied to the effect scale in the earlier architecture study rather than an externally validated threshold of scientific importance; architecture-specific effects can be larger and can change sign.
\item The studies were conducted sequentially. The architecture study was motivated by earlier outcomes, and the pixel-permuted-control task/architecture subset was chosen after inspecting the architecture results. Each follow-up used a protocol specified before evaluation on independent renderer blocks, but the sequence should not be interpreted as one jointly preregistered experiment.
\item Retention versus release compares complete training regimes. Because downstream parameters co-adapt during training, the contrast does not identify the direct causal effect of changing only final first-layer geometry while holding the rest of the trained network fixed.
\item Analyses of individual architecture factors and the later Greenhouse--Geisser, Friedman, filter-similarity, and random-channel analyses include tests specified before recomputation, but some are post-hoc with respect to the original architecture outcomes. They are supporting analyses, not independent prospective confirmation.
\item Filter-template similarity is a geometric score over filter weights. Neither that score nor the visual appearance of filters establishes human interpretability, semantic purity, a unique mechanism, or an identifiable causal abstraction \citep{meloux_2025_mechanistic-identifiability,sutter_2025_causal-abstraction-limits}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Preserving a predefined first-layer filter bank does not determine the patching effect. In controlled CNNs with exact matched counterfactuals, the release-versus-retention comparison changes with intervention size and varies strongly across architectures after the first layer. The same conclusions hold under alternative evaluation metrics, Greenhouse--Geisser correction, permutation tests, and random-channel analyses. A pixel-permuted control experiment on independent renderer blocks further shows that the original two-dimensional edge/corner/ring arrangement changes the patching effect for channels selected on the validation set beyond the bank's Gram matrix, rank, and singular spectrum---but the effect is architecture-dependent, evaluation-metric-dependent, and smaller on average for random channel sets. Filter similarity and patching effects should therefore be reported separately, together with the channel-selection rule used for the intervention.
