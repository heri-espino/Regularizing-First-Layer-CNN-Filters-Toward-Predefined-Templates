\title[Activation Patching in Structured CNNs]{Activation Patching in Structured CNNs: Intervention Size, Architecture, and Additivity}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Heriberto} \sur{Espino Montelongo}}
\affil*[1]{\orgname{Universidad de las Américas Puebla}, \city{San Andrés Cholula}, \state{Puebla}, \country{Mexico}}

\abstract{Activation patching measures how model outputs change when internal activations are replaced, but the measured effect can depend on the intervention itself. We study controlled convolutional networks whose first layer is initialized from a predefined bank of edge, corner, and ring filters. Keeping these filters near the bank produces high weight-space similarity, yet the release-versus-retention patching effect changes with the number of patched channels and with the architecture after the patched layer. A pixel-permuted control shows that the original two-dimensional arrangement changes the centered-logit patching contrast beyond the bank's Gram matrix, rank, and singular spectrum, while the magnitude and interpretation of this difference depend on architecture and metric. We then decompose joint interventions into the sum of singleton channel effects plus a residual. Large intervention-size effects remain in analytically additive networks, showing that such effects do not by themselves imply channel interactions. In deeper networks, including some BatchNorm variants, observed and additive predictions can diverge substantially. The additive reconstruction closely reproduces the architecture-averaged structured-versus-pixel-permuted contrast, while not eliminating architecture-specific discrepancies. In these controlled models, the analysis distinguishes filter similarity, singleton-contribution geometry, transformation by the evaluation metric, and residual departure from singleton superposition.}

\keywords{Explainable AI, activation patching, convolutional neural networks, mechanistic interpretability, predefined filters}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Early convolutional filters are unusually easy to inspect because their weights can still be viewed as spatial kernels. This has motivated fixed wavelet and Gabor representations, Gabor initialization, constrained Gabor layers, and networks built from predefined spatial filters \cite{bruna_2013_scattering-networks,ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization,molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor,linse_2024_predefined-filters,orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. These approaches ask whether useful first-layer structure can be specified or maintained during training.

A separate question is whether preserving that structure gives a predictable functional role. Weight similarity, representational alignment, and influence on model outputs are different properties. Concept-based methods have repeatedly shown that a representation can align with a human-defined concept without being pure, complete, or used in the expected way \cite{kim_2018_tcav,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks,margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. The same distinction applies at the filter level: a learned kernel can remain close to a predefined template without implying that interventions on the corresponding activation channel have a simple effect.

Activation patching offers a direct intervention. An activation from one input is inserted into another forward pass and the resulting output change is measured. However, patching results are sensitive to the source and target inputs, the component being replaced, the number of components replaced, and the output metric \cite{heimersheim_2024_activation-patching,zhang_2024_activation-patching,miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. A multi-channel intervention can also look nonlinear even when channel effects combine additively in logit space, because individual effects can cancel and the evaluation metric itself can be nonlinear.

We study these issues in a controlled vision setting. Every model begins with the same 16-channel first convolution initialized from a bank of edge, corner, and ring filters. In the \emph{retention} condition, an $L_2$ penalty keeps the first layer close to the bank throughout training. In the \emph{release} condition, the same penalty is annealed to zero. Matched counterfactual images make it possible to replace complete first-layer feature maps with activations from another valid image while leaving downstream parameters unchanged during the intervention.

The experiments address four questions. First, does the release-versus-retention patching difference depend on how many channels are patched? Second, does the same first-layer intervention behave differently when the network after the patched layer changes? Third, is the effect specific to the original two-dimensional filter arrangement, or can it be reproduced by a pixel permutation that preserves the bank's Gram matrix, row norms, rank, and singular spectrum? Fourth, how much of the intervention-size effect can be reconstructed from singleton channel effects, and how much requires genuine downstream non-additivity?

The main result is that these questions have different answers. Filter similarity can remain high while patching effects change substantially. Intervention size and architecture both matter. The original spatial arrangement has a measurable effect relative to the pixel-permuted control, but that effect is architecture dependent. Most importantly, dependence on intervention size is not equivalent to interaction among patched channels: exact-additivity controls retain large intervention-size contrasts. The paper therefore treats multi-channel patching as a composition of singleton geometry, metric choice, and residual downstream interaction rather than as a single mechanistic quantity.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Structured first-layer filters}

Predefined and constrained filters have been used to encode known spatial structure before or during CNN training. Wavelet scattering provides a fixed multiscale representation \cite{bruna_2013_scattering-networks}. Gabor-based work has used structured initialization, explicit maintenance of filter structure, and learnable Gabor parameterizations \cite{ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization,molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor,orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. Predefined-filter networks and frozen random convolutions further show that later layers can make effective use of spatial features that are not learned freely \cite{linse_2024_predefined-filters,gaudio_2023_fixed-filters,gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions}. This literature establishes that first-layer structure can persist and remain useful, but it does not imply a unique causal role for individual activation channels.

\subsection{Explanations, interventions, and faithfulness}

Concept-based approaches such as TCAV and concept bottlenecks make internal representations easier to inspect or edit \cite{kim_2018_tcav,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks}. Subsequent work shows that alignment and influence can diverge \cite{margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. Activation patching shifts the focus from decodability to interventions, but the resulting quantity still depends on the experimental definition. In particular, alternative corruption schemes, metrics, and ablation procedures can produce different localization or faithfulness conclusions \cite{zhang_2024_activation-patching,miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. Our contribution is to examine this measurement problem in small CNNs with deliberately structured first-layer filters and to add an explicit singleton-additivity baseline before interpreting intervention-size effects as interactions.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Controlled tasks and filter bank}

We use two $32\times32$ grayscale procedural classification tasks. In \texttt{single\_shape}, the four classes are line, circle, triangle, and square. In \texttt{two\_concepts}, circle and triangle presence define two binary factors and therefore four classes. Rendering varies angle, line width, scale, translation, Gaussian noise, and occasional occlusion. Counterfactual pairs change the class-defining factor while holding nuisance variables fixed.

Every model begins with a bias-free $1\!\to\!16$ convolution with $9\times9$ kernels, same padding, and ReLU. The predefined bank contains eight oriented edge filters, four two-ray corners, and four rings. Each filter is centered and normalized to unit norm. The flattened $16\times81$ bank has numerical rank 10. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the renderer, filter bank, and patching location.

We quantify filter--template similarity by signed cosine similarity after centering each spatial kernel and normalizing it to unit norm. If $\bar w_j$ is learned filter $j$ and $\bar t_m$ is template $m$ after this normalization, the reported nearest-template score is
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{temp}}(W,T)
=
\frac{1}{16}\sum_{j=1}^{16}\max_m \bar w_j^{\top}\bar t_m .
\label{eq:template-similarity}
\end{equation}
A centered kernel with norm below $10^{-12}$ is treated as degenerate rather than assigned an arbitrary cosine. The supplementary morphology analysis additionally reports one-to-one assignment scores; the main text uses the nearest-template score in Eq.~\ref{eq:template-similarity}.

\caption{Controlled experimental system. Representative procedural inputs, the 16 predefined first-layer filters, and the activation-patching intervention. Selected post-ReLU feature maps in a base run are replaced by the corresponding feature maps from a matched counterfactual run.}
\label{fig:overview}

The first-layer kernels are initialized from the predefined bank $W_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Training minimizes cross-entropy plus
\begin{equation}
\lambda(e)\,
\frac{\lVert W-W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2}
     {\lVert W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2}.
\label{eq:regularization}
\end{equation}
Retention keeps $\lambda=1$ throughout 200 epochs. Release keeps $\lambda=1$ through epoch 10, decays it linearly to zero by epoch 80, and then trains without the penalty. These are two complete training regimes; downstream weights are allowed to co-adapt.

Each renderer block contains 128/64/64 independently sampled nuisance contexts for training/validation/test. Every context is instantiated in all four class states, yielding 512/256/256 images per split. Models are trained for 200 epochs with Adam (learning rate $0.003$, batch size 128). The 16-model architecture grid varies total convolutional depth (1, 2, or 4), downstream width (16 or 64), global max versus global average pooling (GMP/GAP), residual connectivity, and downstream BatchNorm. \texttt{Tiny} models pool immediately after the shared first convolution; \texttt{Plain-2} adds one $3\times3$ convolution and \texttt{Plain-4} adds three. Width-64 variants expand the first downstream convolution from 16 to 64 channels; \texttt{Residual-4} uses one skip from the first-layer activation to the third downstream convolution before the final ReLU; and \texttt{BN-2}/\texttt{BN-4} are width-16 plain models with BatchNorm after each downstream convolution. All variants end in global pooling and a four-class linear classifier. Complete layer definitions and parameter counts are given in Supplementary Section S1, \emph{Architecture and training specification}.

\subsection{Activation patching and evaluation}

For every matched pair, the post-ReLU first-layer activations are cached. Channels are ranked on the validation set by the absolute standardized difference in their global-max activations between the two counterfactual states. Test data are not used for ranking. The top $k$ channels are patched for $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$; random same-size channel sets are used as controls.

We report the same output measures throughout the intervention-size and additivity analyses. Centered logits remove the common-logit offset,
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\ell}
=
\ell-\mathrm{mean}(\ell)\mathbf 1.
\label{eq:centered-logits}
\end{equation}
For an output representation $q$---either class probabilities $p=\mathrm{softmax}(\ell)$ or centered logits $\widetilde{\ell}$---define the mean squared discrepancy
\begin{equation}
E_q(a,b)
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left\|q_a^{(i)}-q_b^{(i)}\right\|_2^2,
\label{eq:output-error}
\end{equation}
where $0$, $1$, and $S$ denote the base, matched counterfactual, and patched runs. Fidelity is
\begin{equation}
F_q(S)
=
1-\frac{E_q(S,1)}{E_q(0,1)}.
\label{eq:fidelity}
\end{equation}
The principal normalized metric is $F_{\mathrm{clogit}}$, obtained with $q=\widetilde{\ell}$. Figure~\ref{fig:main-results} also retains the historical probability fidelity $F_{\mathrm{prob}}$. If the summed denominator corresponding to $E_q(0,1)$ is below $10^{-12}$, fidelity is undefined and is not replaced by zero or another finite value.

Because normalization can itself affect interpretation, we additionally use the unnormalized reduction in squared probability error,
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{prob}}(S)
=
E_{\mathrm{prob}}(0,1)-E_{\mathrm{prob}}(S,1).
\label{eq:prob-reduction}
\end{equation}
Thus $F_{\mathrm{clogit}}$ tests movement toward the donor in common-mode-invariant logit space, whereas $R_{\mathrm{prob}}$ measures the absolute reduction in probability-space error.

For any patching metric $M$, let $\Delta^M(k)$ be release minus retention at intervention size $k$. The intervention-size contrast is
\begin{equation}
B^M
=
\frac{\Delta^M(4)+\Delta^M(8)}{2}
-
\frac{\Delta^M(1)+\Delta^M(2)}{2}.
\label{eq:B}
\end{equation}
Positive $B$ means that the release-minus-retention difference is larger on average at $k=4,8$ than at $k=1,2$.

The spatial control applies one common random permutation of the 81 spatial coordinates to every filter in the bank. This destroys the original edge/corner/ring arrangement while preserving the complete Gram matrix, row norms, numerical rank, singular spectrum, and each row's coefficient multiset.

For the spatial-control primary comparison, ordinary difference intervals are 95\% renderer-block intervals. Additivity intervals use 10,000 renderer-block bootstrap resamples after averaging initialization replicates within block unless otherwise stated.

\subsection{Singleton-additive reconstruction}

To separate intervention-size dependence from multi-channel interaction, we re-evaluate the saved architecture and spatial-control checkpoints one channel at a time. Let $P=I-\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top/C$ be the class-logit centering operator and $z=P\ell$. For a matched pair define
\begin{equation}
v=z_1-z_0,\qquad
d_j=z_{\{j\}}-z_0,\qquad
a_S=\sum_{j\in S}d_j,
\label{eq:singleton-changes}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\widehat z_S=z_0+a_S,\qquad
r_S=z_S-\widehat z_S .
\label{eq:additive}
\end{equation}
The observed metrics are evaluated from $z_S$ and the reconstructed metrics from $\widehat z_S$; for probability metrics, softmax is applied after reconstruction. Thus singleton \emph{logit changes}, not singleton metric values, are added.

Cancellation is computed in centered-logit space,
\begin{equation}
K(S)
=
1-
\frac{\|a_S\|_2}
     {\sum_{j\in S}\|d_j\|_2}.
\label{eq:cancellation}
\end{equation}
If the denominator is at most $10^{-12}$, $K(S)$ is reported as undefined rather than set to zero. Cancellation changes the magnitude of the aggregate intervention, but magnitude alone does not determine fidelity. The additive centered-logit error satisfies
\begin{equation}
\|v-a_S\|_2^2
=
\|v\|_2^2
-2v^\top a_S
+\|a_S\|_2^2,
\label{eq:additive-geometry}
\end{equation}
so both alignment with the desired base-to-donor direction, $v^\top a_S$, and aggregate magnitude, $\|a_S\|$, matter. We quantify genuine departure from singleton superposition by the residual $r_S$ and by the aggregate residual-energy ratio
\begin{equation}
\rho(S)
=
\frac{\sum_i\|r_S^{(i)}\|_2^2}
     {\sum_i\|v^{(i)}\|_2^2}.
\label{eq:residual-energy}
\end{equation}
The tiny architectures provide an analytic control because their downstream computation is per-channel pooling followed by a linear classifier; whole-channel replacement is therefore additive in logits up to floating-point error.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Filter similarity does not determine patching behavior}

Retention preserves the intended first-layer structure. On \texttt{two\_concepts}, mean template similarity is 0.944 under retention and 0.574 under release in TinyCNN, while test accuracy remains high in both conditions (98.05\% and 99.45\%). In the corresponding two-layer network, template similarities are 0.999 and 0.896 with accuracies of 99.11\% and 99.26\%. Thus the regularizer strongly changes final filter geometry without producing a comparable separation in ordinary predictive accuracy.

The functional intervention is less simple. In the held-out 20-block TinyCNN test, the original patching contrast is positive,
$B=0.33098$ with a 95\% confidence interval of $[0.27368,0.38827]$. The release-minus-retention differences at $k=1,2,4,8$ are $-0.3459$, $-0.3317$, $-0.0221$, and $+0.0065$, respectively. A single intervention size would therefore give an incomplete description of the comparison.

The same contrast remains positive under the two principal alternative metrics: $B=+0.17888$ for centered-logit fidelity and $B=+0.89441$ for probability-error reduction. Their pointwise curves differ, however, confirming that intervention size and evaluation metric define different views of the same intervention.

\subsection{Downstream architecture strongly changes the effect}

Changing only the network after the patched first layer produces large differences in $B$. Across 16 architectures and 100 independent renderer blocks, the repeated-measures architecture effect is
$F(15,1485)=77.09$ with partial $\eta^2=0.438$ for centered-logit fidelity and
$F(15,1485)=656.70$ with partial $\eta^2=0.869$ for probability-error reduction. The result remains under Greenhouse--Geisser correction, within-block permutation tests, Friedman tests, and random channel orders.

Figure~\ref{fig:main-results} shows the central pattern. Under global-max pooling, most of the Tiny-to-plain change appears when the first downstream convolution is added; adding more plain depth changes less. Under global-average pooling the pattern differs. Residual connections produce comparatively small changes, while BatchNorm and width are strongly context dependent. The result is therefore architecture dependence rather than a universal effect of one architectural ingredient.

\subsection{The spatial arrangement of the filter bank matters}

The pixel-permuted experiment asks whether the measured patching contrast can be attributed only to retaining a fixed low-rank bank. On \texttt{two\_concepts}, the structured-minus-pixel-permuted difference in selected-channel centered-logit $B$, averaged across four architectures, is $+0.05743$ with a 95\% interval of $[+0.05143,+0.06361]$. Its 90\% interval, $[+0.05219,+0.06266]$, lies outside the pre-specified centered-logit equivalence margin $\pm0.01704$. For probability-error reduction the mean difference is $+0.04563$ with a 95\% interval of $[+0.03715,+0.05374]$, but its 90\% interval $[+0.03864,+0.05261]$ lies inside the wider pre-specified equivalence margin $\pm0.06594$. Thus the centered-logit analysis supports a spatial-structure difference under the frozen criterion, whereas the architecture-averaged probability-error effect is nonzero but small enough to satisfy the study-specific equivalence criterion.

The effect is not uniform across architectures. It is positive for \texttt{tiny\_gmp}, \texttt{tiny\_gap}, and \texttt{plain2\_w16\_gap}, but negative for \texttt{plain2\_w16\_gmp}. Random channel subsets yield substantially smaller architecture-averaged differences. Figure~\ref{fig:spatial} shows both the sign reversal and the selected-versus-random comparison.

These results isolate a role for spatial organization beyond simple rank or pairwise filter geometry, while also showing that the sign and magnitude of this role depend on how the first-layer representation is subsequently processed.

\subsection{Intervention-size effects do not imply channel interactions}

The singleton reconstruction changes the interpretation of the earlier $k$-dependence result. In the analytically additive tiny networks, observed and reconstructed curves agree to numerical precision even when $B$ is large. On the primary \texttt{two\_concepts}/\texttt{tiny\_gmp} case, centered-logit fidelity gives $B=+0.14558$ for both observed and additive curves; probability-error reduction gives $B=+0.83151$ for both. Across all $k$, the centered residual-energy ratio remains at floating-point scale: in the release regime it reaches only $8.41\times10^{-14}$ at $k=16$, and in retention $1.45\times10^{-13}$. The full-patch and no-op logit identity errors are exactly zero. The intervention-size effect therefore survives a direct numerical check that the joint logit change is additive.

Cancellation provides one part of the explanation, but not the whole geometry. On \texttt{two\_concepts}/\texttt{tiny\_gmp} in the release regime, $K(S)$ rises from 0.054 at $k=4$ to 0.130 at $k=8$ and 0.193 at $k=16$. This reduces $\|a_S\|$ relative to the sum of singleton magnitudes. Equation~\ref{eq:additive-geometry}, however, shows why cancellation alone is insufficient: fidelity also depends on the alignment term $v^\top a_S$. Large $K(S)$ can therefore coexist with either helpful or unhelpful movement toward the donor, depending on the direction of the surviving sum.

Deeper networks can add a distinct non-additive component. On the primary \texttt{two\_concepts}/\texttt{bn2\_w16\_gap} case, centered-logit $B$ is $-0.13817$ for the observed curve but $+0.04177$ under the additive reconstruction, a sign reversal. Across the same 100 renderer blocks, the paired difference $B_{\mathrm{obs}}-B_{\mathrm{add}}$ is $-0.17994$ with a 95\% paired Student-$t$ interval of $[-0.20082,-0.15906]$. At $k=8$, the residual-energy ratio is 0.190 in release and 0.339 in retention. The secondary \texttt{single\_shape} task contains even larger examples, including \texttt{bn2\_w16\_gap} ($-0.27012$ observed versus $+0.34955$ additive) and \texttt{bn4\_w16\_gap} ($+0.37725$ versus $+1.64910$). These gaps require nonzero $r_S$ and cannot be produced by metric nonlinearity alone. Figure~\ref{fig:additivity-curves} shows the primary Tiny and BN2 cases directly.

The spatial-control comparison is also reproduced closely on average, but the paired comparison makes the scope of that statement clearer. On \texttt{two\_concepts}, the architecture-averaged structured-minus-pixel-permuted centered-logit contrast is $+0.05743$ observed and $+0.05710$ additive. Their paired observed-minus-additive difference across the same 100 renderer blocks is $+0.00033$ with a 95\% paired $t$ interval of $[-0.00074,+0.00140]$. For probability-error reduction, the corresponding contrasts are $+0.04563$ and $+0.04105$, with paired difference $+0.00458$ and 95\% paired $t$ interval $[+0.00202,+0.00714]$. Across the four architectures, the centered-logit observed-minus-additive discrepancy in the spatial contrast ranges from $-0.00473$ to $+0.00604$; for probability error it ranges from $-0.00635$ to $+0.02466$, with the two Tiny architectures essentially at zero. Thus the additive reconstruction reproduces the studied architecture-averaged spatial contrast closely, while some deeper architectures retain non-negligible discrepancies.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The experiments separate several claims that are easy to conflate. First, keeping first-layer kernels close to recognizable spatial templates is a statement about weights. It does not determine how interventions on their activation channels affect model outputs. Second, a patching effect that changes with the number of patched channels is not, by itself, evidence for synergy or another nonlinear interaction. Exact-additivity controls show that singleton geometry and metric choice are sufficient to produce strong intervention-size dependence. Third, genuine non-additivity does occur, but it depends strongly on downstream architecture.

This distinction helps interpret the architecture study. The large differences across CNN variants are not merely different values of the same hidden mechanism. Some architectures largely preserve the additive singleton prediction, whereas others transform joint interventions enough to change the magnitude or even the sign of $B$. Architectures containing BatchNorm provide some of the clearest gaps in the present grid, but this does not localize the cause to BatchNorm itself: with stored statistics in evaluation mode, the BatchNorm transform is affine, and the observed residual can arise from its composition with convolution, ReLU, pooling, and subsequent layers. The analysis identifies architecture-dependent non-additivity without uniquely assigning it to one operation.

The pixel-permuted control adds another layer. The structured bank and its permutation have identical Gram matrices, rank, singular spectra, row norms, and coefficient multisets, but the centered-logit primary analysis does not treat them as equivalent. The probability-error average, by contrast, falls within its wider pre-specified equivalence region. The additive reconstruction reproduces the architecture-averaged spatial contrast closely, especially for centered logits, but architecture-level discrepancies show why this should not be generalized into a claim that spatial structure acts only through singleton contributions. The narrower conclusion is that much of the studied average contrast is already present before a large residual interaction is required.

The study also illustrates a practical lesson for activation-patching work. A mechanistic claim about interactions should be compared with a singleton-additive baseline. Otherwise, curvature caused by vector cancellation or by a nonlinear evaluation metric can be mistaken for model-level interaction. Reporting the intervention size, patched component, source/target construction, channel-selection rule, metric, and architecture makes the measured quantity easier to interpret.

The scope is deliberately controlled. The tasks are synthetic, the networks are small CNNs, and the filters come from one predefined bank. The retention-versus-release comparison also changes the complete training trajectory, so it does not isolate the effect of replacing final first-layer weights while holding the rest of a trained network fixed. Architecture comparisons are not accuracy-matched: they characterize the trained systems in the grid rather than an architecture effect at fixed predictive performance. Patching uses activations from another valid matched image, but the resulting hybrid hidden state can still lie outside the activation distribution encountered during ordinary inference. Channel selection uses global-max first-layer activation for every architecture, including GAP models; random-channel analyses reduce dependence on that ranking choice but do not remove this design feature. The singleton decomposition is post-hoc and reuses the same checkpoints and renderer blocks as the source experiments. Its role is diagnostic: it separates superposition from residual non-additivity, but it does not uniquely identify which downstream layer causes that residual.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Preserving predefined first-layer filters does not determine a unique activation-patching effect. In controlled CNNs, the release-versus-retention comparison depends on intervention size and on the architecture after the patched layer, while a Gram- and rank-matched pixel permutation shows that the original spatial arrangement of the filter bank also matters.

The singleton decomposition provides the main mechanistic clarification. Large intervention-size effects remain in networks that are exactly additive in logits, so dependence on $k$ should not be interpreted as evidence of channel interaction without an explicit additive baseline. Deeper networks can introduce substantial residual non-additivity, and the amount of that residual is architecture dependent. For the spatial-control study, the singleton reconstruction reproduces the architecture-averaged structured-versus-pixel-permuted contrast closely, while architecture-specific gaps constrain broader mechanistic interpretations.

Taken together, the results argue for separating structural alignment, singleton-effect geometry, metric dependence, and downstream interaction when interpreting activation-patching experiments.
