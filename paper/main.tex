\title[When Do Activation-Patching Effects Add Up?]{When Do Activation-Patching Effects Add Up? Evidence from Controlled CNNs}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Heriberto} \sur{Espino Montelongo}}
\affil*[1]{\orgname{Universidad de las Am\'ericas Puebla}, \city{San Andr\'es Cholula}, \state{Puebla}, \country{Mexico}}

\abstract{Can the effect of patching several activation channels be predicted from the effects of patching them individually? We examine this question in controlled convolutional networks initialized with predefined edge, corner, and ring filters. Two training regimes either retain these filters through regularization or release them by annealing the penalty. Joint interventions reveal two distinct outcomes. In networks with per-channel pooling and a linear classifier, the release--retention contrast changes substantially with intervention size even though individual logit changes reconstruct the joint effect to numerical precision. In a deeper network, the same additive reconstruction predicts a positive intervention-size contrast while the observed contrast is negative: $+0.042$ versus $-0.138$. A 100-block comparison across 16 architectures shows that the gap between individual and joint effects depends on the downstream network. A separate spatial control permutes filter pixels while preserving the bank's Gram matrix, rank, and singular spectrum. The original arrangement changes the patching contrast, but its architecture-averaged centered-logit difference is closely reproduced by individual effects. These results distinguish changes caused by the geometry of individual contributions and the evaluation metric from departures from logit additivity. In the studied CNNs, intervention-size dependence alone is insufficient evidence of channel interaction, while assuming additivity can reverse the interpretation of a training-regime comparison.}

\keywords{Activation patching, mechanistic interpretability, convolutional neural networks, additivity, predefined filters}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

An activation channel can have a small effect when patched alone and a different role when patched with other channels. Conversely, a joint patching curve can change sharply as channels are added even when their effects combine exactly in logit space. These possibilities create a practical problem: a change in a measured patching score does not, by itself, identify a change in how components interact. To interpret a joint intervention, we need to know what the individual interventions already predict.

Activation patching replaces an internal activation with its value from another input and measures the resulting output change. It is widely used to investigate the functional roles of model components, but its conclusions depend on the source and target inputs, the patched components, and the output metric \citep{heimersheim_2024_activation-patching,zhang_2024_activation-patching,miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. The difficulty extends beyond choosing a metric. \citet{zhang_2024_activation-patching} compare simultaneous multi-layer patches with sums of individual-layer effects and find discrepancies. \citet{vaidyanathan_2026_multiple-mediators} derive interaction terms in causal mediation and multi-component patching, showing why effects measured separately need not predict a circuit's joint behavior. The existence of such interactions is therefore established; the empirical question is when they are needed to explain a particular patching result.

We study that question in small CNNs whose first layer begins with predefined edge, corner, and ring filters. This setting makes both the weights and the intervention explicit. Structured spatial filters have a long history in wavelet representations, Gabor initialization, and constrained convolutional layers \citep{bruna_2013_scattering-networks,ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization,molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor,linse_2024_predefined-filters,orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. They offer recognizable first-layer structure, but a recognizable kernel does not establish how its activation channel contributes to a prediction. Weight similarity, representation of a concept, and influence on the output are different properties \citep{kim_2018_tcav,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks,margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}.

Our experiments compare two training regimes. A constant penalty retains the first-layer filters near their predefined bank; annealing the penalty releases them during training. Matched counterfactual images hold rendering nuisance variables fixed while changing a class-defining factor. We patch complete post-ReLU first-layer feature maps, vary the number of replaced channels, and evaluate how closely the patched output approaches the counterfactual output. The main quantity is a contrast in the release-minus-retention difference between smaller and larger interventions. We then reconstruct every joint intervention by summing its individual channel changes in centered-logit space and applying the evaluation metric to that reconstructed output.

The comparison exposes two outcomes with different interpretations. In the smallest networks, individual changes reconstruct the joint output to numerical precision, yet the intervention-size contrast remains substantial. In a deeper network, the additive reconstruction predicts a positive contrast while the observed contrast is negative. Thus, size dependence can occur without a logit interaction residual, but a residual can also change the sign of the conclusion. A separate pixel-permuted bank preserves pairwise filter inner products and spectral properties while changing spatial arrangement. Its architecture-averaged contrast is closely reproduced by individual effects, showing that a spatial-structure difference need not be attributed primarily to joint non-additivity.

We combine three types of evidence to distinguish these explanations in structured CNNs. Exact-additivity architectures establish what can happen without a residual; a 16-architecture grid measures where that explanation fails; and a Gram-preserving spatial control tests what filter similarity leaves unspecified. Together, these experiments determine when the sum of individual logit effects explains the reported joint patching contrast and when relying on that sum changes its interpretation.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Structured filters and functional interpretation}

Wavelet scattering builds fixed multiscale representations \citep{bruna_2013_scattering-networks}. Gabor-based CNNs use structured initialization, maintenance of filter structure, or learnable filter parameterizations \citep{ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization,molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor,orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. Predefined spatial filters and frozen random convolutions also provide useful features for downstream learning \citep{linse_2024_predefined-filters,gaudio_2023_fixed-filters,gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions}. Our study uses predefined filters to make first-layer structure controllable and inspectable. It evaluates the effect of intervening on the resulting channels rather than proposing another filter parameterization.

Concept-based explanations offer a related distinction between an inspectable representation and its functional use. TCAV and concept bottlenecks connect model representations to specified concepts \citep{kim_2018_tcav,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks}, while subsequent work examines the limits of concept alignment and its relationship to influence \citep{margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. Here, similarity to an edge, corner, or ring template describes the filter weights. The patching experiment measures a separate property of the trained network.

\subsection{Patching metrics and joint interventions}

\citet{zhang_2024_activation-patching} show that corruption methods and evaluation metrics can alter localization results. Their sliding-window analysis explicitly compares joint multi-layer patching with sums of single-layer effects. \citet{heimersheim_2024_activation-patching} discuss the interpretation of patching evidence and the different behavior of logit- and probability-based metrics. \citet{miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness} document sensitivity of circuit faithfulness to ablation methodology. These studies motivate checking both the intervention and its measurement before assigning a mechanistic interpretation.

Our comparison adds individual \emph{logit changes}, rather than individual scalar patching scores. This distinction matters because squared reconstruction error and softmax can transform an additive output change into a non-additive score. Reconstructing the logits first allows the same metric transformation to act on both the predicted and observed joint outputs.

\subsection{Interaction effects and the scope of our baseline}

\citet{vaidyanathan_2026_multiple-mediators} distinguish effects associated with individual mediators and their bypasses from cross-component interactions in jointly patched sets. Their analysis links these quantities to ranking failures and faithfulness variability in transformer circuits and gives a second-order account under a smooth downstream map. Their multi-component decomposition is a close antecedent to any attempt to compare individual and joint interventions.

We use a fixed-base, vector-valued reconstruction in centered-logit space. Its residual aggregates departure from the sum of individual channel changes; it does not estimate their single-mediator interaction term, recover pure path-specific effects, or resolve pairwise and higher-order terms separately. Individual channel effects can themselves depend on the unpatched context. The empirical addition is to compare this aggregate baseline with trained CNNs, including an architecture with exact whole-channel additivity, a broader architecture grid, and a spatially permuted filter-bank control. For ReLU networks and finite channel replacements, the reconstruction also avoids requiring a local smoothness approximation.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Controlled inputs and training regimes}

We use two $32\times32$ grayscale procedural classification tasks. In \texttt{single\_shape}, the four classes are line, circle, triangle, and square. In \texttt{two\_concepts}, circle and triangle presence define two binary factors and four classes. Rendering varies angle, line width, scale, translation, Gaussian noise, and occasional occlusion. A counterfactual pair changes a class-defining factor while holding nuisance variables fixed. The primary presentation uses \texttt{two\_concepts}; \texttt{single\_shape} tests whether the same qualitative phenomena occur under a second renderer.

Each renderer block contains 128, 64, and 64 independently sampled nuisance contexts for training, validation, and testing, respectively. Instantiating every context in all four class states gives 512, 256, and 256 images in the corresponding splits. A block is the inferential unit: initialization replicates within a block are averaged before statistical analysis.

All models begin with a bias-free $1\!\to\!16$ convolution with $9\times9$ kernels, same padding, and ReLU. The predefined bank contains eight oriented edge filters, four two-ray corners, and four rings. Each filter is centered and normalized to unit norm; the flattened $16\times81$ bank has numerical rank 10. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows the inputs, filters, and intervention location.

Training minimizes cross-entropy plus a normalized first-layer penalty,
\begin{equation}
\lambda(e)\,
\frac{\lVert W-W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2}
     {\lVert W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2},
\label{eq:regularization}
\end{equation}
where $W_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the initialization bank and $e$ is the epoch. Retention keeps $\lambda=1$ throughout 200 epochs. Release keeps $\lambda=1$ through epoch 10, decreases it linearly to zero by epoch 80, and then trains without the penalty. Both use Adam with learning rate $0.003$ and batch size 128. These are complete training regimes: downstream parameters can co-adapt with the first layer.

Filter--template similarity is signed cosine similarity after centering each spatial kernel and normalizing it to unit norm. For normalized learned filter $\bar w_j$ and template $\bar t_m$, the nearest-template score is
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{temp}}(W,T)
=\frac{1}{16}\sum_{j=1}^{16}\max_m \bar w_j^{\top}\bar t_m.
\label{eq:template-similarity}
\end{equation}
A centered kernel with norm below $10^{-12}$ is treated as degenerate. Supplementary morphology summaries additionally report one-to-one assignment scores.

\subsection{Architectures and spatial control}

The 16-model grid varies total convolutional depth (1, 2, or 4), downstream width (16 or 64), global max versus global average pooling (GMP/GAP), residual connectivity, and downstream BatchNorm. Tiny models pool immediately after the first convolution. Plain-2 adds one $3\times3$ convolution; Plain-4 adds three. Width-64 variants expand the first downstream convolution from 16 to 64 channels. Residual-4 has one skip from the first-layer activation to the third downstream convolution before its final ReLU. BN-2 and BN-4 are width-16 plain models with BatchNorm after each downstream convolution. All variants end in global pooling and a four-class linear classifier. Complete layer definitions and parameter counts are in Supplementary Section S1, \emph{Architecture and training specification}.

The spatial control applies one common random permutation to the 81 coordinates of every filter. Writing the bank as $T\in\mathbb R^{16\times81}$ and the permutation matrix as $Q$, the permuted bank satisfies
\begin{equation}
(TQ)(TQ)^\top=TT^\top.
\label{eq:gram-control}
\end{equation}
It also preserves row norms, rank, singular spectrum, and each row's coefficient multiset, while changing the original two-dimensional arrangement. Both banks are evaluated under retention and release. The comparison therefore tests two matched bank-based training regimes, with downstream learning allowed in each; it is not a replacement of final kernels in an otherwise fixed trained network.

\subsection{Patching and output measures}

For each matched pair, we cache the post-ReLU first-layer activations. Channels are ranked using the validation-set absolute standardized difference in their global-max activations between counterfactual states. This ranking is used for every architecture, including GAP models. Test data are not used for selection. The original analyses patch the top $k$ channels for $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$, with random same-size channel sets as controls. The reconstruction evaluates $k=0,1,\ldots,16$.

Let $\ell\in\mathbb R^C$ be the class logits, with $C=4$, and define centered logits
\begin{equation}
z=P\ell,\qquad P=I-\frac{\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top}{C}.
\label{eq:centered-logits}
\end{equation}
Centering removes the common-logit offset and leaves softmax probabilities unchanged. Subscripts $0$, $1$, and $S$ denote the base, counterfactual donor, and run patched on channel set $S$. For either centered logits or class probabilities, $q\in\{z,p\}$ with $p=\mathrm{softmax}(\ell)$, define
\begin{equation}
E_q(a,b)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\lVert q_a^{(i)}-q_b^{(i)}\rVert_2^2,
\label{eq:output-error}
\end{equation}
where $i$ indexes evaluated pairs. Fidelity is
\begin{equation}
F_q(S)=1-\frac{E_q(S,1)}{E_q(0,1)}.
\label{eq:fidelity}
\end{equation}
We denote centered-logit fidelity by $F_{\mathrm{clogit}}$ and the historical probability fidelity by $F_{\mathrm{prob}}$. Fidelity is undefined if its corresponding summed denominator is below $10^{-12}$. It is not clipped to $[0,1]$: negative values indicate greater discrepancy from the donor than in the base run.

To check whether normalization drives the comparison, we also report unnormalized probability-error reduction,
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{prob}}(S)=E_{\mathrm{prob}}(0,1)-E_{\mathrm{prob}}(S,1).
\label{eq:prob-reduction}
\end{equation}
Thus $F_{\mathrm{clogit}}$ measures relative recovery of the donor's centered logits, while $R_{\mathrm{prob}}$ measures an absolute reduction in probability-space error.

For a metric $M$, write $\Delta^M(k)=M_{\mathrm{release}}(k)-M_{\mathrm{retention}}(k)$. The intervention-size contrast is
\begin{equation}
B^M=\frac{\Delta^M(4)+\Delta^M(8)}{2}
   -\frac{\Delta^M(1)+\Delta^M(2)}{2}.
\label{eq:B}
\end{equation}
Positive $B$ means that the release-minus-retention difference is larger on average at $k=4,8$ than at $k=1,2$. It does not require release to outperform retention at every size, nor does it imply a monotone patching curve. A sign reversal between observed and reconstructed $B$ reverses this size-dependent training-regime comparison, not necessarily classification accuracy or an individual channel's effect.

\subsection{Predicting joint outputs from individual patches}

For a fixed trained model and input pair, the single-channel change is measured against the same base run. Define
\begin{equation}
v=z_1-z_0,\qquad d_j=z_{\{j\}}-z_0,\qquad
a_S=\sum_{j\in S}d_j,
\label{eq:singleton-changes}
\end{equation}
and reconstruct the joint output as
\begin{equation}
\widehat z_S=z_0+a_S,\qquad r_S=z_S-\widehat z_S.
\label{eq:additive}
\end{equation}
Observed metrics use $z_S$; reconstructed metrics use $\widehat z_S$. For probability metrics, we apply softmax \emph{after} summing the logit changes. We never sum single-channel fidelities or probability-error reductions to predict a joint metric.

Even when $r_S=0$, fidelity need not be additive in channel set size. The additive centered-logit error is
\begin{equation}
\lVert v-a_S\rVert_2^2
=\lVert v\rVert_2^2-2v^\top a_S+\lVert a_S\rVert_2^2.
\label{eq:additive-geometry}
\end{equation}
The sum's alignment with the donor direction and its magnitude both matter. Expanding $\lVert a_S\rVert_2^2$ produces cross-products between individual logit changes even in an exactly additive network. Such cross-products belong to the squared-error measurement; they do not establish non-additivity of the network's logit response.

We summarize cancellation by
\begin{equation}
K(S)=1-\frac{\lVert a_S\rVert_2}
                 {\sum_{j\in S}\lVert d_j\rVert_2},
\label{eq:cancellation}
\end{equation}
reported as undefined when the denominator is at most $10^{-12}$. A larger $K(S)$ indicates more cancellation, but does not determine whether the surviving sum moves toward the donor. Residual energy is
\begin{equation}
\rho(S)=\frac{\sum_i\lVert r_S^{(i)}\rVert_2^2}
                  {\sum_i\lVert v^{(i)}\rVert_2^2}.
\label{eq:residual-energy}
\end{equation}
It measures departure from singleton superposition relative to the donor--base change; it is not a proportion of explained variance and need not be bounded by one.

Tiny architectures supply an exact control. If $h_j$ is channel $j$, $\phi_j$ is its independent spatial pooling operation, and $A_j$ is its classifier column, their logits have the form $\ell(h)=b+\sum_j A_j\phi_j(h_j)$. Replacing complete channels in $S$ changes this output by
\begin{equation}
\ell_S-\ell_0
=\sum_{j\in S}A_j\bigl[\phi_j(h_{1,j})-\phi_j(h_{0,j})\bigr]
=\sum_{j\in S}(\ell_{\{j\}}-\ell_0).
\label{eq:tiny-additivity}
\end{equation}
Centering is linear, so the same identity holds for $z$. Global-max pooling can be nonlinear within a channel and still satisfy this whole-channel identity. Exact additivity here follows from the architecture; it is an analytic control, not an unexpected empirical discovery.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Intervention-size dependence survives exact logit additivity}

The retention regime maintains recognizable filters, but filter similarity does not settle the patching comparison. On \texttt{two\_concepts}, TinyCNN's mean template similarity is 0.944 under retention and 0.574 under release; test accuracies are 98.05\% and 99.45\%. In the corresponding two-layer network, similarities are 0.999 and 0.896, with accuracies of 99.11\% and 99.26\%. The regularizer therefore produces a clear difference in filter geometry while both regimes classify the procedural images accurately.

In the held-out 20-block TinyCNN test, the release-minus-retention probability-fidelity differences at $k=1,2,4,8$ are $-0.3459$, $-0.3317$, $-0.0221$, and $+0.0065$. The size contrast is $B=+0.33098$, with a 95\% interval of $[0.27368,0.38827]$. Centered-logit fidelity and unnormalized probability-error reduction also give positive contrasts, $+0.17888$ and $+0.89441$, respectively. The outcome at one intervention size is therefore insufficient to describe the training-regime comparison.

The reconstruction establishes what this pattern does \emph{not} require. In the later 100-block Tiny-GMP analysis on \texttt{two\_concepts}, observed and reconstructed $B$ are both $+0.14558$ for centered-logit fidelity and both $+0.83151$ for probability-error reduction. The full curves agree to numerical precision (Fig.~\ref{fig:additivity-curves}a). At $k=16$, residual energy is only $8.41\times10^{-14}$ under release and $1.45\times10^{-13}$ under retention; across sizes it remains at floating-point scale. The no-op and full-patch logit identity errors are exactly zero. These values come from the reused 100-block study, not the earlier 20-block confirmatory test.

Cancellation increases as more channels are patched: in the release regime, $K(S)$ rises from 0.054 at $k=4$ to 0.130 at $k=8$ and 0.193 at $k=16$. This describes a reduction in the magnitude of the sum relative to individual magnitudes. Whether that reduction improves fidelity also depends on the alignment term in Eq.~\ref{eq:additive-geometry}. The central observation is therefore not that cancellation alone explains the curve, but that individual-effect geometry and the metric can produce substantial size dependence with no detectable logit interaction residual.

\subsection{A joint effect can reverse the additive prediction}

The additive explanation has a clear limit. In BN2-GAP on \texttt{two\_concepts}, the observed centered-logit contrast is $B_{\mathrm{obs}}=-0.13817$, whereas the individual-effect reconstruction predicts $B_{\mathrm{add}}=+0.04177$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:additivity-curves}b). Their paired difference across the same 100 renderer blocks is $-0.17994$, with a 95\% Student-$t$ interval of $[-0.20082,-0.15906]$. At $k=8$, residual energy is 0.190 under release and 0.339 under retention. The reconstruction consequently gives the wrong sign for how the release--retention comparison changes between smaller and larger patches.

The secondary \texttt{single\_shape} task supplies further failures: BN2-GAP gives $-0.27012$ observed versus $+0.34955$ reconstructed, and BN4-GAP gives $+0.37725$ versus $+1.64910$. These discrepancies cannot arise from applying a nonlinear metric to identical logits: observed and reconstructed outputs already differ before metric evaluation. They establish departure from singleton superposition for these finite interventions, without identifying a unique downstream operation responsible for it.

The architecture grid puts these examples in context. Across 16 architectures and 100 independent renderer blocks, the repeated-measures architecture effect on $B$ is $F(15,1485)=77.09$, with partial $\eta^2=0.438$, for centered-logit fidelity, and $F(15,1485)=656.70$, with partial $\eta^2=0.869$, for probability-error reduction. Architecture differences remain under Greenhouse--Geisser correction, within-block permutation tests, Friedman tests, and random channel orders. Figure~\ref{fig:main-results} shows the original size curves and the architecture-level contrasts; Fig.~\ref{fig:additivity}a compares those contrasts with their individual-effect reconstructions.

Under GMP, much of the Tiny-to-plain change appears when the first downstream convolution is added; further plain depth changes less. GAP gives a different pattern. Residual connections produce comparatively small changes, while width and BatchNorm effects depend on the surrounding architecture. The grid supports architecture-dependent behavior, rather than a universal depth effect or an attribution of the residual specifically to BatchNorm.

\subsection{Matching the Gram matrix does not match the spatial contrast}

The pixel-permuted bank asks whether the original patching result can be explained solely by retaining a bank with the same rank and pairwise filter geometry. On \texttt{two\_concepts}, the structured-minus-permuted difference in selected-channel centered-logit $B$, averaged across four architectures, is $+0.05743$. Its 95\% paired Student-$t$ interval is $[+0.05116,+0.06369]$, and its 90\% interval $[+0.05219,+0.06266]$ lies outside the pre-specified equivalence margin $\pm0.01704$. Identical bank Gram matrices therefore do not yield equivalent centered-logit contrasts under this criterion.

Probability-error reduction gives a different assessment of magnitude. The mean difference is $+0.04563$, with a 95\% paired Student-$t$ interval of $[+0.03728,+0.05397]$. Its 90\% interval, $[+0.03864,+0.05261]$, lies within the wider margin $\pm0.06594$. This average is nonzero but satisfies the study-specific equivalence criterion. The two outcomes concern different scales and margins; they do not establish a universally meaningful or negligible spatial effect.

Spatial arrangement also has no uniform direction of effect. The selected-channel difference is positive in Tiny-GMP, Tiny-GAP, and Plain-2-GAP, but negative in Plain-2-GMP. Random subsets give smaller architecture-averaged differences (Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}). The control therefore identifies sensitivity to the original arrangement in these training regimes, rather than a general advantage of visually recognizable filters.

\subsection{Individual effects largely reproduce the average spatial difference}

Does the spatial contrast require a large joint-interaction contribution? For the architecture-averaged centered-logit comparison, the observed structured-minus-permuted difference is $+0.05743$ and the reconstruction gives $+0.05710$. The paired observed-minus-reconstructed difference is $+0.00033$, with a 95\% Student-$t$ interval of $[-0.00074,+0.00140]$. Individual logit effects, passed through the same fidelity metric, closely reproduce the average contrast (Fig.~\ref{fig:additivity}b). An interval containing zero is not a separate proof of equivalence, but it makes the scale of the remaining average discrepancy explicit.

For probability-error reduction, the contrasts are $+0.04563$ observed and $+0.04105$ reconstructed. Their paired difference is $+0.00458$, with a 95\% interval of $[+0.00202,+0.00714]$. Across individual architectures, observed-minus-reconstructed spatial discrepancies range from $-0.00473$ to $+0.00604$ for centered logits and from $-0.00635$ to $+0.02466$ for probability error; both Tiny variants are essentially at zero. The average agreement therefore does not imply agreement in every architecture. It shows that much of this particular spatial comparison is already captured by individual-effect reconstruction.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The useful distinction is between an explanation of the \emph{score} and an explanation of the \emph{joint logit response}. A squared-error fidelity can vary strongly with patch size because individual changes point in different directions and because the metric contains cross-products. Probability metrics additionally transform the summed logits through softmax. These transformations remain when logit additivity is exact. The Tiny control demonstrates that interpreting size dependence as evidence of a channel-interaction mechanism would be unwarranted in that architecture.

The BN2-GAP result demonstrates the opposite failure: assuming that individual effects suffice can reverse the size contrast. Its residual is not just a numerical deviation from a convenient baseline; it changes the comparison between training regimes. Residual energy alone, however, does not determine whether a joint patch improves fidelity. Since $z_S=z_0+a_S+r_S$, the donor error is $\lVert v-a_S-r_S\rVert_2^2$. The residual's direction relative to $v-a_S$, as well as its magnitude, matters. A nonzero residual is evidence against singleton superposition for the evaluated patches, not a label for a beneficial synergy or a uniquely identified mechanism.

This interpretation is consistent with the interaction analysis of \citet{vaidyanathan_2026_multiple-mediators}: separately measured components can fail to predict collective behavior. Our results add an empirical comparison in which some trained systems permit exact logit reconstruction while others produce consequential discrepancies. A zero residual for the evaluated channel sets does not establish context-independent causal importance, nor does it exclude higher-order terms that cancel in aggregate. The reconstruction is a diagnostic for a specified intervention, not a substitute for combinatorial or pathway analysis when a mechanistic claim requires it.

The spatial control extends the same reasoning to filter structure. The original and permuted banks have matched inner-product geometry, but differ in their two-dimensional arrangement and in the trained responses built on that arrangement. Their patching contrasts can differ without a large average residual from individual-effect reconstruction. Weight-space similarity consequently leaves unspecified both the individual functional responses and the downstream combination of those responses. The experiment establishes this distinction for the bank and permutation studied; it does not rank spatially structured filters as universally more interpretable.

In practice, a claim about channel interactions can be evaluated by reconstructing joint logits from individual interventions, applying the original metric to that reconstruction, and reporting the paired discrepancy alongside the observed contrast. This comparison distinguishes a metric-transformed sum from a joint response that the sum cannot predict. Intervention size, donor/base construction, channel-selection rule, and downstream architecture remain part of the claim being tested.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}

The tasks are synthetic, the networks are small, and the spatial control uses one predefined bank and one common permutation. These choices support controlled comparisons, but do not establish the frequency or magnitude of the phenomena in large models trained on natural images. Retention and release also change complete training trajectories, with downstream co-adaptation. Architecture comparisons are not accuracy-matched and characterize the trained systems rather than an architecture effect at fixed predictive performance.

The largest reconstruction discrepancies in this grid include BatchNorm architectures, but they cannot be assigned specifically to BatchNorm. With stored statistics in evaluation mode, BatchNorm is affine; non-additivity can arise from its composition with channel mixing, ReLU, pooling, and later layers. A layer-specific causal explanation would require further interventions.

Matched donors are valid rendered images, but the patched hybrid state may still fall outside ordinary activation distributions. Selection uses global-max first-layer activations even in GAP models; random-channel analyses reduce dependence on this ranking without removing it. Finally, the reconstruction is post-hoc and uses the original saved blocks. Its paired comparison diagnoses those results; it is not a new confirmatory replication. These boundaries define the claims that the present evidence supports.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Individual activation patches can be sufficient to explain a joint patching contrast, or insufficient even to predict its sign. In controlled CNNs, an intervention-size contrast persists under exact logit additivity, while a deeper architecture produces a residual that reverses the additive prediction. A Gram-preserving filter permutation further changes the patching contrast, although individual effects closely reproduce its architecture-averaged centered-logit difference.

The resulting distinction is practical: a size-dependent score does not establish a channel interaction, and an additive prediction should be checked before it is used to interpret a joint intervention. Comparing observed joint outputs with reconstructed individual effects makes both possibilities visible. It also gives a more specific account of the studied results than filter similarity or patching magnitude alone.
