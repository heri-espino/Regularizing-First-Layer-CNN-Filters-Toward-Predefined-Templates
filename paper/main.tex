\title[When Do Activation-Patching Effects Add Up?]{When Do Activation-Patching Effects Add Up? Evidence from Controlled CNNs}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Heriberto} \sur{Espino Montelongo}}
\affil*[1]{\orgname{Universidad de las Am\'ericas Puebla}, \city{San Andr\'es Cholula}, \state{Puebla}, \country{Mexico}}

\abstract{Can the effect of patching several activation channels be predicted from the effects of patching them individually? We examine this question in controlled convolutional networks initialized with predefined edge, corner, and ring filters. Two training regimes either retain these filters through regularization or release them by annealing the penalty. We find two distinct outcomes. In networks with per-channel pooling and a linear classifier, the release--retention contrast changes substantially with intervention size even though individual logit changes reconstruct the joint effect to numerical precision. In a deeper network, the same additive reconstruction predicts a positive intervention-size contrast while the observed contrast is negative: $+0.042$ versus $-0.138$. A 100-block comparison across 16 architectures shows that the gap between individual and joint effects depends on the downstream network. A separate spatial control permutes filter pixels while preserving the bank's Gram matrix, rank, and singular values. The original arrangement changes the patching contrast, but individual effects closely reproduce the average centered-logit difference across architectures. These results separate changes in patching scores that individual logit changes and the evaluation metric can explain from changes that require departures from logit additivity. In the studied CNNs, intervention-size dependence alone is insufficient evidence of channel interaction, while assuming additivity can give the wrong sign for the intervention-size contrast between training regimes.}

\keywords{Activation patching, mechanistic interpretability, convolutional neural networks, additivity, predefined filters}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

An activation channel can have a small effect when patched alone and a different role when patched with other channels. Conversely, a joint patching curve can change sharply as channels are added even when their effects combine exactly in logit space. These possibilities create a practical problem: a change in a measured patching score does not, by itself, identify a change in how components interact. To interpret a joint intervention, we need to know what the individual interventions already predict.

Activation patching replaces an internal activation with its value from another input and measures the resulting output change. It is widely used to investigate the functional roles of model components, but its conclusions depend on the source and target inputs, the patched components, and the evaluation metric \citep{heimersheim_2024_activation-patching,zhang_2024_activation-patching,miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. The difficulty extends beyond choosing a metric. \citet{zhang_2024_activation-patching} compare simultaneous multi-layer patches with sums of individual-layer effects and find discrepancies. \citet{vaidyanathan_2026_multiple-mediators} derive interaction terms in causal mediation and multi-component patching, showing why effects measured separately need not predict a circuit's joint behavior. These interactions are already established. We ask when they are needed to explain a particular patching result.

We study that question in small CNNs whose first layer begins with predefined edge, corner, and ring filters. This setting lets us control the initial filters and the channels we patch. Structured spatial filters have a long history in wavelet representations, Gabor initialization, and constrained convolutional layers \citep{bruna_2013_scattering-networks,ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization,molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor,linse_2024_predefined-filters,orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. These filters have recognizable spatial patterns, but a recognizable kernel does not establish how its activation channel contributes to a prediction. Weight similarity, representation of a concept, and influence on the output are different properties \citep{kim_2018_tcav,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks,margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}.

Our experiments compare two training regimes. A constant penalty retains the first-layer filters near their predefined bank; annealing the penalty releases them during training. Matched counterfactual images change a class-defining factor while keeping rendering conditions such as angle, scale, and noise fixed. We patch complete post-ReLU first-layer feature maps, vary the number of replaced channels, and evaluate how closely the patched output approaches the counterfactual output. We measure how the release-minus-retention difference changes between smaller and larger interventions. We sum the centered-logit changes from individual channel patches to predict each joint output, then evaluate that output with the same metric.

The comparison gives two different outcomes. In the smallest networks, individual changes reconstruct the joint output to numerical precision, yet the intervention-size contrast remains substantial. In a deeper network, the additive reconstruction predicts a positive contrast while the observed contrast is negative. Thus, the score can depend on intervention size even when joint logit changes equal the sum of individual changes. When the joint logit change differs from this sum, the discrepancy can reverse the sign of the contrast. A separate control permutes filter pixels while preserving pairwise filter inner products and singular values. Individual effects closely reproduce the contrast averaged across architectures. Most of this average spatial difference can therefore be explained without a large departure from logit additivity.

We combine three types of evidence to distinguish these explanations in structured CNNs. Architectures with exact logit additivity show what can happen when individual changes add up. A comparison of 16 architectures shows where that prediction fails. A spatial control that preserves the Gram matrix tests whether matched filter inner products imply the same patching contrast. Together, these experiments determine when the sum of individual logit effects explains the reported joint patching contrast and when relying on that sum changes its interpretation.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Structured filters and functional interpretation}

Wavelet scattering builds fixed multiscale representations \citep{bruna_2013_scattering-networks}. Gabor-based CNNs initialize filters with Gabor kernels, maintain their structure during training, or learn their parameters \citep{ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization,molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor,orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. Predefined spatial filters and frozen random convolutions also provide useful features for downstream learning \citep{linse_2024_predefined-filters,gaudio_2023_fixed-filters,gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions}. Our study uses predefined filters to make first-layer structure controllable and inspectable. It evaluates the effect of intervening on the resulting channels rather than proposing another filter parameterization.

Concept-based explanations make a similar distinction between representing a concept and using it to produce an output. TCAV and concept bottlenecks connect model representations to specified concepts \citep{kim_2018_tcav,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks}, while subsequent work examines the limits of concept alignment and its relationship to influence \citep{margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. Here, similarity to an edge, corner, or ring template describes the filter weights. The patching experiment measures a separate property of the trained network.

\subsection{Patching metrics and joint interventions}

\citet{zhang_2024_activation-patching} show that corruption methods and evaluation metrics can alter localization results. Their sliding-window analysis explicitly compares joint multi-layer patching with sums of single-layer effects. \citet{heimersheim_2024_activation-patching} discuss the interpretation of patching evidence and the different behavior of logit- and probability-based metrics. \citet{miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness} document sensitivity of circuit faithfulness to ablation methodology. These studies motivate checking both the intervention and its measurement before assigning a mechanistic interpretation.

Our comparison adds individual \emph{logit changes}, rather than individual scalar patching scores. This distinction matters because squared reconstruction error and softmax can transform an additive output change into a non-additive score. By reconstructing the logits first, we can apply the same metric to the predicted and observed joint outputs.

\subsection{Interaction effects and the scope of our baseline}

\citet{vaidyanathan_2026_multiple-mediators} distinguish effects associated with individual mediators and their bypasses from cross-component interactions in jointly patched sets. Their analysis connects these terms to errors in ranking components and variation in transformer circuit faithfulness. It also gives a second-order approximation when the downstream function is smooth. Their decomposition directly informs comparisons of individual and joint interventions.

We reconstruct the centered-logit vector by measuring every individual channel change against the same base run and summing those changes. The residual is the difference between this prediction and the observed joint output. This residual does not estimate the single-mediator interaction term defined in that work, isolate effects along specific causal paths, or separate pairwise from higher-order interactions. Individual channel effects can themselves depend on the activations left unpatched. We compare this prediction with observed outputs in trained CNNs. The experiments include architectures with exact additivity when complete channels are replaced, a grid of 16 architectures, and a pixel-permuted filter bank. The reconstruction uses the actual output changes from channel replacements, so it does not require a local smoothness approximation for the ReLU network.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Controlled inputs and training regimes}

We use two $32\times32$ grayscale procedural classification tasks. In \texttt{single\_shape}, the four classes are line, circle, triangle, and square. In \texttt{two\_concepts}, circle and triangle presence define two binary factors and four classes. Rendering varies angle, line width, scale, translation, Gaussian noise, and occasional occlusion. A counterfactual pair changes a class-defining factor while keeping the other rendering conditions fixed. We focus on \texttt{two\_concepts} and use \texttt{single\_shape} to check whether similar patterns occur with a second renderer.

Each renderer block contains 128 training, 64 validation, and 64 test contexts sampled independently. A context specifies rendering conditions such as angle, scale, and noise. Rendering all four classes in every context gives 512, 256, and 256 images in the corresponding splits. Statistical analysis treats each block as one independent observation after averaging its initialization replicates.

All models begin with a bias-free $1\!\to\!16$ convolution with $9\times9$ kernels, same padding, and ReLU. The predefined bank contains eight oriented edge filters, four two-ray corners, and four rings. Each filter is centered and normalized to unit norm; the flattened $16\times81$ bank has numerical rank 10. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows the inputs, filters, and intervention location.

Training minimizes cross-entropy plus a normalized first-layer penalty,
\begin{equation}
\lambda(e)\,
\frac{\lVert W-W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2}
     {\lVert W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2},
\label{eq:regularization}
\end{equation}
where $W_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the initialization bank and $e$ is the epoch. Retention keeps $\lambda=1$ throughout 200 epochs. Release keeps $\lambda=1$ through epoch 10, decreases it linearly to zero by epoch 80, and then trains without the penalty. Both regimes train the entire network with Adam, learning rate $0.003$, and batch size 128. Downstream parameters can therefore change along with the first layer.

Filter--template similarity is signed cosine similarity after centering each spatial kernel and normalizing it to unit norm. For normalized learned filter $\bar w_j$ and template $\bar t_m$, the nearest-template score is
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{temp}}(W,T)
=\frac{1}{16}\sum_{j=1}^{16}\max_m \bar w_j^{\top}\bar t_m.
\label{eq:template-similarity}
\end{equation}
A centered kernel with norm below $10^{-12}$ is treated as degenerate. The supplementary filter-similarity summaries also report scores obtained by matching each learned filter to a different template.

\subsection{Architectures and spatial control}

The grid of 16 architectures varies total convolutional depth (1, 2, or 4), downstream width (16 or 64), global max versus global average pooling (GMP/GAP), residual connections, and downstream BatchNorm. Tiny models pool immediately after the first convolution. Plain-2 adds one $3\times3$ convolution; Plain-4 adds three. Width-64 variants expand the first downstream convolution from 16 to 64 channels. Residual-4 has one skip from the first-layer activation to the third downstream convolution before its final ReLU. BN-2 and BN-4 are width-16 plain models with BatchNorm after each downstream convolution. All variants end in global pooling and a four-class linear classifier. Complete layer definitions and parameter counts are in Supplementary Section S1, \emph{Architecture and training specification}.

The spatial control applies one common random permutation to the 81 coordinates of every filter. Writing the bank as $T\in\mathbb R^{16\times81}$ and the permutation matrix as $Q$, the permuted bank satisfies
\begin{equation}
(TQ)(TQ)^\top=TT^\top.
\label{eq:gram-control}
\end{equation}
It also preserves row norms, rank, singular values, and the coefficients in each filter, including how often each value occurs, while changing their two-dimensional arrangement. For each bank, we train networks under both retention and release, allowing downstream parameters to adapt. We therefore compare trained networks rather than replacing filters in a fixed network.

\subsection{Patching and output measures}

For each matched pair, we cache the post-ReLU first-layer activations. We rank channels on the validation set by the absolute standardized difference between their global-max activations in the two counterfactual states. This ranking is used for every architecture, including GAP models. Test data are not used for selection. The original analyses patch the top $k$ channels for $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$, with random same-size channel sets as controls. The reconstruction evaluates $k=0,1,\ldots,16$.

Let $\ell\in\mathbb R^C$ be the class logits, with $C=4$, and define centered logits
\begin{equation}
z=P\ell,\qquad P=I-\frac{\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top}{C}.
\label{eq:centered-logits}
\end{equation}
Centering subtracts the mean logit from every class logit and leaves softmax probabilities unchanged. Subscripts $0$, $1$, and $S$ denote the base, counterfactual donor, and run patched on channel set $S$. For either centered logits or class probabilities, $q\in\{z,p\}$ with $p=\mathrm{softmax}(\ell)$, define
\begin{equation}
E_q(a,b)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\lVert q_a^{(i)}-q_b^{(i)}\rVert_2^2,
\label{eq:output-error}
\end{equation}
where $i$ indexes evaluated pairs. Fidelity is
\begin{equation}
F_q(S)=1-\frac{E_q(S,1)}{E_q(0,1)}.
\label{eq:fidelity}
\end{equation}
We denote centered-logit fidelity by $F_{\mathrm{clogit}}$ and the probability fidelity used in the initial analysis by $F_{\mathrm{prob}}$. Fidelity is undefined when the sum of squared base--donor differences for the corresponding metric is below $10^{-12}$. Values are not clipped to $[0,1]$: a negative value means the patched output is farther from the donor than the base output is.

To check whether normalization drives the comparison, we also report unnormalized probability-error reduction,
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{prob}}(S)=E_{\mathrm{prob}}(0,1)-E_{\mathrm{prob}}(S,1).
\label{eq:prob-reduction}
\end{equation}
Thus $F_{\mathrm{clogit}}$ measures relative recovery of the donor's centered logits, while $R_{\mathrm{prob}}$ measures an absolute reduction in probability-space error.

For a metric $M$, write $\Delta^M(k)=M_{\mathrm{release}}(k)-M_{\mathrm{retention}}(k)$. The intervention-size contrast is
\begin{equation}
B^M=\frac{\Delta^M(4)+\Delta^M(8)}{2}
   -\frac{\Delta^M(1)+\Delta^M(2)}{2}.
\label{eq:B}
\end{equation}
Positive $B$ means that the release-minus-retention difference is larger on average at $k=4,8$ than at $k=1,2$. It does not require release to outperform retention at every size, nor does it imply a monotone patching curve. Opposite signs of observed and reconstructed $B$ give opposite conclusions about how this difference changes with intervention size. They need not imply opposite conclusions about classification accuracy or an individual channel's effect.

\subsection{Predicting joint outputs from individual patches}

For a fixed trained model and input pair, the single-channel change is measured against the same base run. Define
\begin{equation}
v=z_1-z_0,\qquad d_j=z_{\{j\}}-z_0,\qquad
a_S=\sum_{j\in S}d_j,
\label{eq:singleton-changes}
\end{equation}
and reconstruct the joint output as
\begin{equation}
\widehat z_S=z_0+a_S,\qquad r_S=z_S-\widehat z_S.
\label{eq:additive}
\end{equation}
Observed metrics use $z_S$; reconstructed metrics use $\widehat z_S$. For probability metrics, we apply softmax \emph{after} summing the logit changes. We never sum single-channel fidelities or probability-error reductions to predict a joint metric.

Even when $r_S=0$, the joint fidelity need not equal the sum of single-channel fidelities. The additive centered-logit error is
\begin{equation}
\lVert v-a_S\rVert_2^2
=\lVert v\rVert_2^2-2v^\top a_S+\lVert a_S\rVert_2^2.
\label{eq:additive-geometry}
\end{equation}
Both the magnitude of the summed change and its alignment with the base-to-donor change matter. Expanding $\lVert a_S\rVert_2^2$ produces cross-products between individual logit changes even in an exactly additive network. These cross-products arise from the squared-error metric, even when the network's logit changes are additive.

We summarize cancellation by
\begin{equation}
K(S)=1-\frac{\lVert a_S\rVert_2}
                 {\sum_{j\in S}\lVert d_j\rVert_2},
\label{eq:cancellation}
\end{equation}
reported as undefined when the denominator is at most $10^{-12}$. A larger $K(S)$ indicates more cancellation, but does not determine whether the summed change moves the output toward the donor. Residual energy is
\begin{equation}
\rho(S)=\frac{\sum_i\lVert r_S^{(i)}\rVert_2^2}
                  {\sum_i\lVert v^{(i)}\rVert_2^2}.
\label{eq:residual-energy}
\end{equation}
It measures how far joint logit changes depart from the sum of single-channel changes, relative to the donor--base change. It is not a proportion of explained variance and can exceed one.

Tiny architectures provide a control with exact logit additivity. If $h_j$ is channel $j$, $\phi_j$ is its independent spatial pooling operation, and $A_j$ is its classifier column, their logits have the form $\ell(h)=b+\sum_j A_j\phi_j(h_j)$. Replacing complete channels in $S$ changes this output by
\begin{equation}
\ell_S-\ell_0
=\sum_{j\in S}A_j\bigl[\phi_j(h_{1,j})-\phi_j(h_{0,j})\bigr]
=\sum_{j\in S}(\ell_{\{j\}}-\ell_0).
\label{eq:tiny-additivity}
\end{equation}
Centering is linear, so the same identity holds for $z$. Global-max pooling can be nonlinear within a channel and still satisfy this identity when complete channels are replaced. Exact additivity follows directly from the architecture, which makes these models a known control.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Intervention-size dependence under exact logit additivity}

Retention keeps filters close to the templates, but filter similarity alone does not predict their patching effects. On \texttt{two\_concepts}, TinyCNN's mean template similarity is 0.944 under retention and 0.574 under release; test accuracies are 98.05\% and 99.45\%. In the corresponding two-layer network, similarities are 0.999 and 0.896, with accuracies of 99.11\% and 99.26\%. The regularizer therefore changes how closely the filters match the templates, while both regimes classify the procedural images accurately.

In the held-out 20-block TinyCNN test, the release-minus-retention probability-fidelity differences at $k=1,2,4,8$ are $-0.3459$, $-0.3317$, $-0.0221$, and $+0.0065$. The size contrast is $B=+0.33098$, with a 95\% interval of $[0.27368,0.38827]$. Centered-logit fidelity and unnormalized probability-error reduction also give positive contrasts, $+0.17888$ and $+0.89441$, respectively. The comparison between training regimes therefore depends on which intervention size is evaluated.

Tiny-GMP shows how this size dependence can occur under exact logit additivity. In the later 100-block Tiny-GMP analysis on \texttt{two\_concepts}, observed and reconstructed $B$ are both $+0.14558$ for centered-logit fidelity and both $+0.83151$ for probability-error reduction. The full curves agree to numerical precision (Fig.~\ref{fig:additivity-curves}a). At $k=16$, residual energy is only $8.41\times10^{-14}$ under release and $1.45\times10^{-13}$ under retention; across sizes it remains at numerical precision. The no-op patch reproduces the base logits, and the full patch reproduces the donor logits, both with zero error. These values come from the reused 100-block study, not the earlier 20-block confirmatory test.

Cancellation increases as more channels are patched: in the release regime, $K(S)$ rises from 0.054 at $k=4$ to 0.130 at $k=8$ and 0.193 at $k=16$. This describes a reduction in the magnitude of the sum relative to individual magnitudes. Whether that reduction improves fidelity also depends on the alignment term in Eq.~\ref{eq:additive-geometry}. Cancellation alone does not explain the curve. The directions and magnitudes of individual logit changes, together with the evaluation metric, can make the score depend strongly on intervention size even when the logit changes add up to numerical precision.

\subsection{A joint effect can reverse the additive prediction}

Individual logit changes do not always predict the joint response. In BN2-GAP on \texttt{two\_concepts}, the observed centered-logit contrast is $B_{\mathrm{obs}}=-0.13817$, whereas the reconstruction from individual logit changes predicts $B_{\mathrm{add}}=+0.04177$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:additivity-curves}b). Their paired difference across the same 100 renderer blocks is $-0.17994$, with a 95\% Student-$t$ interval of $[-0.20082,-0.15906]$. At $k=8$, residual energy is 0.190 under release and 0.339 under retention. The reconstruction consequently gives the wrong sign for how the release--retention comparison changes between smaller and larger patches.

The reconstruction also fails on the secondary \texttt{single\_shape} task: BN2-GAP gives $-0.27012$ observed versus $+0.34955$ reconstructed, and BN4-GAP gives $+0.37725$ versus $+1.64910$. These discrepancies cannot arise from applying a nonlinear metric to identical logits: observed and reconstructed outputs already differ before metric evaluation. For these channel replacements, joint logit changes differ from the sum of single-channel changes. This result does not identify which downstream operation causes the difference.

The architecture grid puts these examples in context. Across 16 architectures and 100 independent renderer blocks, repeated-measures tests show differences in $B$ across architectures. For centered-logit fidelity, $F(15,1485)=77.09$, with partial $\eta^2=0.438$. For probability-error reduction, $F(15,1485)=656.70$, with partial $\eta^2=0.869$. Architecture differences remain under Greenhouse--Geisser correction, within-block permutation tests, Friedman tests, and random channel orders. Figure~\ref{fig:main-results} shows the original size curves and the architecture-level contrasts; Fig.~\ref{fig:additivity}a compares those contrasts with predictions from individual channel patches.

Under GMP, adding the first downstream convolution accounts for much of the change from Tiny to plain models. Adding further convolutions has a smaller effect. GAP gives a different pattern. Residual connections produce comparatively small changes. The effects of width and BatchNorm depend on the other architectural choices. The grid does not establish a universal effect of depth or show that BatchNorm alone causes the residual.

\subsection{Filter banks with identical Gram matrices give different patching contrasts}

The pixel-permuted control tests whether preserving the bank's rank and pairwise filter inner products is enough to reproduce the original patching contrast. For channels selected on the validation set in \texttt{two\_concepts}, centered-logit $B$ is $+0.05743$ higher for the structured bank than for the permuted bank, averaged across four architectures. Its 95\% paired Student-$t$ interval is $[+0.05116,+0.06369]$, and its 90\% interval $[+0.05219,+0.06266]$ lies outside the pre-specified equivalence margin $\pm0.01704$. Identical bank Gram matrices therefore do not yield equivalent centered-logit contrasts under this criterion.

The probability-error result meets the equivalence criterion. The mean difference is $+0.04563$, with a 95\% paired Student-$t$ interval of $[+0.03728,+0.05397]$. Its 90\% interval, $[+0.03864,+0.05261]$, lies within the wider margin $\pm0.06594$. The 95\% interval excludes zero, while the 90\% interval satisfies the study-specific equivalence criterion.

The metrics have different scales and equivalence margins. These results do not establish that the spatial effect is generally important or negligible.

The direction of the spatial effect also depends on architecture. For channels selected on the validation set, the difference is positive in Tiny-GMP, Tiny-GAP, and Plain-2-GAP, but negative in Plain-2-GMP. Random subsets give smaller differences averaged across architectures (Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}). The control therefore identifies sensitivity to the original arrangement in these training regimes, rather than a general advantage of visually recognizable filters.

\subsection{Individual effects largely reproduce the average spatial difference}

Does the spatial contrast require a large departure from logit additivity? For centered-logit $B$ averaged across architectures, the observed difference between the structured and permuted banks is $+0.05743$. The reconstruction gives $+0.05710$. The paired observed-minus-reconstructed difference is $+0.00033$, with a 95\% Student-$t$ interval of $[-0.00074,+0.00140]$. Individual logit effects, passed through the same fidelity metric, closely reproduce the average contrast (Fig.~\ref{fig:additivity}b). The interval contains zero, but this alone does not establish equivalence.

For probability-error reduction, the contrasts are $+0.04563$ observed and $+0.04105$ reconstructed. Their paired difference is $+0.00458$, with a 95\% interval of $[+0.00202,+0.00714]$. Across individual architectures, differences between observed and reconstructed spatial contrasts range from $-0.00473$ to $+0.00604$ for centered logits and from $-0.00635$ to $+0.02466$ for probability error; both Tiny variants are essentially at zero. The average agreement therefore does not imply agreement in every architecture. It shows that summing individual logit changes explains much of this particular spatial comparison.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

To interpret a joint patch, we must distinguish changes in the \emph{score} from departures from additivity in the \emph{joint logit response}. A squared-error fidelity can vary strongly with patch size because individual changes point in different directions and because the metric contains cross-products. Probability metrics additionally transform the summed logits through softmax. These transformations remain when logit additivity is exact. The Tiny control shows that size dependence alone is insufficient evidence of channel interaction in that architecture.

The BN2-GAP result demonstrates the opposite failure: assuming that individual effects suffice can reverse the size contrast. The reconstruction error changes the sign of the reported comparison between training regimes. Residual energy alone, however, does not determine whether a joint patch improves fidelity. Since $z_S=z_0+a_S+r_S$, the donor error is $\lVert v-a_S-r_S\rVert_2^2$. Both the residual's magnitude and its direction relative to $v-a_S$ matter. A nonzero residual shows that the individual logit changes do not add up for the evaluated patches. It does not show that the interaction improves fidelity or identify a unique mechanism.

This interpretation is consistent with the interaction analysis of \citet{vaidyanathan_2026_multiple-mediators}: separately measured components can fail to predict collective behavior. Our comparison includes trained networks where individual patches reconstruct the joint logits exactly, and others where the reconstruction error changes the sign of the reported contrast. A zero residual for the evaluated channel sets does not establish context-independent causal importance, nor does it exclude higher-order interactions whose contributions cancel. The reconstruction checks additivity for the specified intervention. Claims about specific mechanisms may still require tests of other channel combinations or causal paths.

The spatial control extends the same reasoning to filter structure. The original and permuted banks have the same pairwise filter inner products. Their two-dimensional arrangements differ, as do the responses learned from them. Their patching contrasts can differ even when individual logit changes closely predict the joint outputs on average. Filter similarity alone therefore does not determine the effect of an individual channel patch or how those effects combine in the downstream network. The experiment establishes this distinction for the bank and permutation studied; it does not rank spatially structured filters as universally more interpretable.

In practice, reconstruct the joint logits from individual interventions, apply the original metric, and compare the reconstructed contrast with the observed contrast using a paired analysis. This comparison shows whether the sum of individual logit changes, evaluated with the same metric, predicts the joint response. The interpretation still depends on intervention size, how donor and base inputs are constructed, how channels are selected, and the downstream architecture.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}

The tasks are synthetic, the networks are small, and the spatial control uses one predefined bank and one common permutation. These choices support controlled comparisons, but do not establish the frequency or magnitude of the phenomena in large models trained on natural images. Retention and release train the entire network differently, allowing downstream parameters to adapt along with the first layer. We do not match classification accuracy across architectures. The comparisons describe the trained networks, rather than isolating an architecture effect at fixed accuracy.

The largest reconstruction discrepancies in this grid include BatchNorm architectures, but they cannot be assigned specifically to BatchNorm. With stored statistics in evaluation mode, BatchNorm is affine; non-additivity can arise from its composition with channel mixing, ReLU, pooling, and later layers. A layer-specific causal explanation would require further interventions.

Matched donors are valid rendered images, but the patched hybrid state may still fall outside ordinary activation distributions. Selection uses global-max first-layer activations even in GAP models; random-channel analyses reduce dependence on this ranking without removing it. Finally, the reconstruction is post-hoc and uses the original saved blocks. Its paired comparison checks additivity in those existing results; it is not a new confirmatory replication. These boundaries define the claims that the present evidence supports.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Individual activation patches can be sufficient to explain a joint patching contrast, or insufficient even to predict its sign. In controlled CNNs, an intervention-size contrast persists under exact logit additivity, while a deeper architecture produces a residual that reverses the additive prediction. Permuting filter pixels while preserving the Gram matrix also changes the patching contrast. Individual effects closely reproduce the centered-logit difference averaged across architectures.

The practical implication is: a size-dependent score does not establish a channel interaction, and an additive prediction should be checked before it is used to interpret a joint intervention. Comparing observed joint outputs with predictions from individual patches distinguishes these two cases. It also gives a more specific account of the studied results than filter similarity or patching magnitude alone.
