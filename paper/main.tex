\begin{abstract}
Activation patching measures how model outputs change when internal activations are replaced, but the measured effect need not be determined by the visible structure of the intervened representation. We study controlled CNNs whose first layer starts from the same predefined bank of edge, corner, and ring filters. Constant regularization retains that bank, whereas an annealed penalty releases it during training. On matched counterfactual image pairs, a pre-specified study on 20 independent renderer blocks shows that the release-versus-retention difference changes with the number of patched channels; the same intervention-size contrast remains positive under centered-logit fidelity and unnormalized probability-error reduction. A separate study on 100 independent renderer blocks and 16 CNN architectures shows substantial architecture dependence. A further 100-block control replaces the original filter bank by a common spatial permutation that preserves its Gram matrix, rank, singular spectrum, row norms, and coefficient multisets. The spatial arrangement changes the selected-channel patching comparison, but the magnitude and even direction of that change depend on architecture and evaluation metric. These results separate two questions that are easy to conflate: whether filters remain close to a predefined bank, and how interventions on the resulting activations propagate through the trained network.\footnote{ChatGPT (OpenAI) was used as an assistive tool during experimental planning, code development and debugging, manuscript organization, and language editing. All experimental protocols, analyses, interpretations, and manuscript text were reviewed by the authors, who take full responsibility for the work.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Early convolutional filters are unusually accessible objects in an otherwise distributed computation. This has motivated fixed wavelet representations, Gabor initialization, explicit maintenance of Gabor structure, learnable Gabor filters, and networks built from predefined spatial filters \citep{bruna_2013_scattering-networks,ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization,molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor,linse_2024_predefined-filters,orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. Fixed and even random spatial kernels can also remain useful when later layers learn how to recombine their outputs \citep{gaudio_2023_fixed-filters,gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions}. This literature establishes that first-layer structure can be preserved or imposed. It does not establish what an intervention on the corresponding activations should do.

That distinction is important because representation and influence are different objects. Concept-based methods measure or impose alignment between internal states and human-defined concepts \citep{bau_2017_network-dissection,kim_2018_tcav,chen_2020_concept-whitening,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks}, while later work shows that apparent alignment can coexist with concept leakage, ambiguous semantics, layer dependence, or weak causal influence \citep{margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,havasi_2022_concept-leakage,marconato_2022_glancenets,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. Interpretation-motivated regularization can also alter predictive behavior and robustness rather than merely producing more recognizable internal structure \citep{noack_2021_interpretability-robustness}. A filter that remains close to a predefined template therefore need not have a simple or stable intervention effect.

Activation patching directly probes such effects by replacing an internal activation from one run with the corresponding activation from another. However, the resulting quantity is defined jointly by the model and the intervention procedure. Corruption choice, patch direction, component granularity, intervention size, and output metric can all change the measured result \citep{heimersheim_2024_activation-patching,zhang_2024_activation-patching,miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. More broadly, explanation methods can disagree across models and methods \citep{gunasekaran_2024_model-class-reliance}, and explanations can be altered in ways that change what a user infers about a model \citep{schneider_2024_deceptive-xai}. These observations motivate precise claims about what an intervention experiment measures rather than treating an explanation score as an intrinsic property of a representation.

We use procedural image tasks to isolate this problem. Every model begins with the same 16-channel first convolution and the same predefined edge/corner/ring bank. One training condition keeps the filters near that bank; another starts identically and then releases the constraint. Matched counterfactual image pairs provide in-distribution donor activations. We then vary the number of patched first-layer channels, the downstream CNN architecture, and the spatial arrangement of the reference bank while keeping the intervention location fixed.

The paper makes three empirical contributions. First, preserving first-layer filter structure does not yield an intervention-size-invariant release-versus-retention comparison, and the pre-specified channel-count contrast persists under two alternative output metrics. Second, the same first-layer intervention produces markedly different contrasts across 16 downstream architectures. Third, a Gram/rank/spectrum-matched pixel permutation shows that the original two-dimensional arrangement of the reference filters contributes to the measured effect for validation-selected channels, but not with a universal sign or metric-independent magnitude. The goal is not to identify a unique mechanism from these experiments, but to delimit what can and cannot be inferred from structured filters and activation patching in a controlled vision setting.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Predefined and structured spatial filters.}
Hand-designed spatial filters predate modern CNNs, and scattering networks show that useful invariant image representations can be built from fixed wavelet operations \citep{bruna_2013_scattering-networks}. More recent work has used Gabor banks as initialization \citep{ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization}, maintained Gabor structure during optimization \citep{molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure}, constrained learnable Gabor parameters \citep{wang_2024_learnable-gabor}, and tracked degeneration of initially structured filters during training \citep{orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. Predefined-filter networks and fixed-filter networks further show that arbitrary learned spatial kernels are not always necessary for competitive prediction \citep{linse_2024_predefined-filters,gaudio_2023_fixed-filters}, while random frozen convolutions can work when learned linear combinations remain expressive \citep{gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions}. Our use of template similarity is therefore a structural measurement only; it is not treated as a semantic or mechanistic explanation.

\paragraph{Concept alignment and functional influence.}
Network Dissection, TCAV, Concept Whitening, and concept bottlenecks attach human-facing meaning to internal representations in different ways \citep{bau_2017_network-dissection,kim_2018_tcav,chen_2020_concept-whitening,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks}. Their limitations are informative for our setting. Concept bottlenecks can encode information beyond their intended concepts \citep{margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,havasi_2022_concept-leakage}; concept vectors can depend on layer, probe set, entanglement, and spatial position \citep{nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability}; and decodable information need not be the information that functionally changes model outputs \citep{sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. We apply the same separation one level lower: weight-space similarity to a filter bank is not equivalent to the downstream effect of replacing activation channels.

\paragraph{Intervention methodology and explanation multiplicity.}
Activation patching and related interchange interventions localize effects by replacing internal states rather than only decoding them \citep{geiger_2025_causal-abstraction,heimersheim_2024_activation-patching}. Zhang and Nanda show that corruption and metric choices can change localization results \citep{zhang_2024_activation-patching}; Miller et al.\ show analogous sensitivity for circuit-faithfulness measurements \citep{miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. These are not merely implementation details: different procedures instantiate different counterfactual questions. The same general caution appears outside mechanistic interpretability. Gunasekaran et al.\ document explanation multiplicity across near-equivalent models and feature-importance methods \citep{gunasekaran_2024_model-class-reliance}, while Schneider et al.\ study how altered explanations can remain persuasive despite reduced faithfulness to the underlying decision process \citep{schneider_2024_deceptive-xai}. Our contribution is a controlled vision study in which the first-layer prior, intervention size, architecture, metric, and spatial filter arrangement can be varied separately.

\section{Experimental design}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Controlled tasks and first-layer filter prior}\label{sec:setup}

We use two $32\times32$ grayscale procedural classification tasks with matched counterfactual pairs. Each renderer block contains 512 training, 256 validation, and 256 test images. Rendering varies angle, line width, scale, translation, Gaussian noise, and occasional occlusion. In \texttt{single\_shape}, the four classes are line, circle, triangle, and square. In \texttt{two\_concepts}, circle and triangle presence define two binary factors and four classes; a counterfactual changes one factor while holding the other factor and nuisance variables fixed. Earlier experiments showed clearer intervention-size dependence on \texttt{two\_concepts}; later follow-ups therefore use it as the primary task while retaining \texttt{single\_shape} as a secondary scope check.

Every model begins with a bias-free $1\!\to\!16$ convolution with $9\times9$ kernels, same padding, and ReLU. The predefined bank contains eight oriented edges, four two-ray corners, and four rings. Each template is centered and unit norm; the flattened $16\times81$ bank has numerical rank 10. For learned filter $W_i$ and template $T_j$, structural similarity is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:template-similarity}
A=\frac{1}{16}\sum_i\max_j\langle \widehat W_i,\widehat T_j\rangle,
\end{equation}
where the inner product is signed cosine similarity after centering and normalizing each filter. One-to-one matching is retained as a secondary structural check.

Template-based models initialize the first convolution from $W_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and optimize
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:training-objective}
\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}+
\lambda(e)\frac{\lVert W-W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2}
{\lVert W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2}.
\end{equation}
\emph{Retention} keeps $\lambda(e)=1$ for all 200 epochs. \emph{Release} keeps $\lambda=1$ through epoch 10, decays linearly to zero by epoch 80, and remains zero thereafter. Adam uses learning rate $0.003$ and batch size 128. Retention versus release is a comparison between complete training regimes: downstream parameters co-adapt while the first layer evolves.

The architecture study keeps the first convolution and intervention tensor fixed and varies only the network after that tensor. Sixteen variants manipulate pooling, convolutional depth, width, residual connectivity, and downstream BatchNorm. The later spatial-control study uses four architectures selected from the completed architecture study: \texttt{tiny\_gmp}, \texttt{tiny\_gap}, \texttt{plain2\_w16\_gmp}, and \texttt{plain2\_w16\_gap}. That subset was specified before the spatial-control outcomes were evaluated.

\subsection{Activation patching and evaluation}\label{sec:patching}

For each matched pair, we cache the post-ReLU first-layer tensors from the base and counterfactual images. A patched run starts from the base image and replaces selected feature maps with the corresponding counterfactual maps. No test-set quantity is used to select channels. Channels are ranked on validation data by the absolute standardized difference in global-max first-layer activation for the factor changed by the counterfactual. The same ranking rule is used for every architecture. Eight random channel orders are evaluated as a control.

Let
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:probability-error}
E_{\mathrm{prob}}(a,b)=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert p_a^{(i)}-p_b^{(i)}\rVert_2^2 .
\end{equation}
The historical normalized probability score is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:probability-fidelity}
F_{\mathrm{prob}}(S)=
1-\frac{E_{\mathrm{prob}}(S,1)}{E_{\mathrm{prob}}(0,1)} .
\end{equation}
Because its denominator is model-specific, later analyses also use centered logits,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:centered-logits}
\widetilde{\ell}=\ell-\mathrm{mean}(\ell)\mathbf 1,
\end{equation}
with the same normalized reconstruction form, and the unnormalized probability error reduction
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:probability-error-reduction}
R_{\mathrm{prob}}(S)=
E_{\mathrm{prob}}(0,1)-E_{\mathrm{prob}}(S,1).
\end{equation}

For metric $M$, the within-block release-minus-retention difference at intervention size $k$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:treatment-difference}
\Delta_b^M(k)=M_b^{\mathrm{release}}(k)-M_b^{\mathrm{retention}}(k),
\end{equation}
and the pre-specified small-versus-larger intervention contrast is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:channel-count-contrast}
B_b^M=
\frac{\Delta_b^M(4)+\Delta_b^M(8)}{2}
-\frac{\Delta_b^M(1)+\Delta_b^M(2)}{2}.
\end{equation}
A positive $B$ means only that the release-minus-retention difference is larger on average at $k=4,8$ than at $k=1,2$.

The additive reconstruction analysis was specified after the source outcomes were known and is being computed from saved checkpoints without retraining. For centered logits, let $z_0$, $z_1$, and $z_S$ be the base, donor, and jointly patched states. For singleton channel $j$, define $d_j=z_{\{j\}}-z_0$ and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:additive-reconstruction}
\widehat z_S=z_0+\sum_{j\in S}d_j,
\qquad
r_S=z_S-\widehat z_S .
\end{equation}
The frozen analysis separates singleton contributions and their cancellation, non-additivity of the network tail through $r_S$, and nonlinearity introduced by the evaluation metric. Metric curvature is not labeled a model interaction, and a nonzero residual is not by itself interpreted as synergy or redundancy. No result from this analysis is assumed below; its results section is intentionally left for insertion after the frozen computation finishes.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Retention preserves the predefined filter structure}\label{sec:structural}

Constant regularization does what it was designed to do: it keeps the first-layer filters close to the reference bank. On the larger schedule/task sensitivity sample for \texttt{two\_concepts}, TinyCNN similarity is 0.944 under retention and 0.574 under release; the corresponding two-layer values are 0.999 and 0.896. Test accuracy remains high in those two GMP models. The lower panel of \cref{fig:system} makes the structural difference visible, but the result used below is the replicate-level similarity score rather than visual inspection. One-to-one matching gives the same qualitative conclusion and is reported in the Supplementary Material.

\subsection{The release-versus-retention comparison changes with intervention size}\label{sec:intervention-size}

The main intervention-size pattern is visible before considering any omnibus statistic: retention and release produce different reconstruction curves, and their separation changes as more channels are replaced (\cref{fig:intervention-size}). In the pre-specified 20-block TinyCNN experiment, the historical normalized-probability contrast is $\bar B=0.33098$ with 95\% CI $[0.27368,0.38827]$. The same contrast remains positive under centered-logit fidelity, $B=0.17888$ with 95\% CI $[0.12727,0.23049]$, and under unnormalized probability error reduction, $B=0.89441$ with 95\% CI $[0.78630,1.00252]$.

The metrics do not produce identical pointwise curves. In particular, the normalized probability score is close to zero for the release-minus-retention difference at larger $k$ even when unnormalized probability error reduction is strongly positive. Thus the robust statement is about the change between smaller and larger interventions, not about a metric-independent value at every $k$.

\subsection{Downstream architecture changes the same first-layer comparison}\label{sec:architecture}

Holding the patched tensor, training comparison, channel ranking, counterfactual construction, and evaluation metrics fixed does not make $B$ architecture-invariant. Across 16 CNN variants on 100 independent renderer blocks, architecture explains substantial within-block variation: centered-logit fidelity gives $F_{15,1485}=77.09$ with partial $\eta^2=0.438$, while probability error reduction gives $F_{15,1485}=656.70$ with partial $\eta^2=0.869$.

\Cref{fig:architecture} shows the full architecture set rather than only the largest contrasts. Under global-max pooling, most of the Tiny-to-plain shift appears when the first downstream convolution is added; further plain depth changes comparatively little. Under global-average pooling, the pattern differs, and GAP-versus-GMP contrasts reverse direction across some backbones. Residual-versus-plain depth-4 contrasts are comparatively small, while BatchNorm effects depend on depth, pooling, and metric. The fresh TinyGMP-minus-Plain2-GMP bridge is $+0.16226$ in centered-logit $B$ and $+0.88960$ in probability-error $B$. The architecture result also remains under permutation, Friedman, sphericity-corrected, and random-channel analyses reported in the Supplementary Material.

\subsection{The two-dimensional filter arrangement contributes to the measured effect}\label{sec:anchor-specificity}

The spatial-control experiment asks whether regularizing toward the original edge/corner/ring arrangement differs from regularizing toward a bank with the same basic linear-algebraic structure. Its primary control applies one common permutation of the 81 spatial coordinates to every template. This preserves the complete Gram matrix, row norms, numerical rank, singular spectrum, and row coefficient multisets while destroying the original two-dimensional arrangement.

For validation-selected channels, the structured-minus-permuted difference in centered-logit $B$, averaged across the four architectures, is $+0.05743$ with 95\% CI $[+0.05116,+0.06369]$. Its 90\% interval lies outside the pre-specified equivalence margin of $\pm0.01704$. The corresponding probability-error difference is $+0.04563$ with 95\% CI $[+0.03728,+0.05397]$, but its 90\% interval lies within the wider pre-specified margin of $\pm0.06594$. These are deliberately different conclusions: the spatial arrangement is detectable under both metrics, but only the centered-logit average lies outside its study-specific equivalence region.

The architecture-specific view is equally important. The structured-minus-permuted effect is positive in three of the four architectures and negative in \texttt{plain2\_w16\_gmp} under both metrics (\cref{fig:anchor-specificity}). Random-channel averages are much smaller than selected-channel averages. The result therefore does not support a universal benefit of the original templates; it supports a conditional effect of their spatial arrangement on this intervention measurement.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

The experiments separate structural retention from intervention behavior. Constant regularization can keep the first-layer kernels very close to a predefined bank, yet that structural fact does not specify how replacing the corresponding activation channels will affect the final output. The release-versus-retention difference changes with intervention size, and the same first-layer intervention propagates differently through different downstream architectures. This is consistent with the broader distinction between representational alignment and functional influence in concept-based and mechanistic interpretability \citep{margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}.

The spatial-control result narrows the claim further. A common pixel permutation preserves several global algebraic properties of the filter bank while destroying its two-dimensional organization. The resulting change in $B$ shows that spatial arrangement can matter beyond Gram matrix, rank, and singular spectrum. But it is not a universal positive effect: one architecture reverses sign, the architecture-averaged equivalence conclusion differs across metrics, and random-channel effects are smaller. The most defensible interpretation is therefore conditional: spatial structure contributes to the measured patching comparison in selected channels, and downstream computation determines how that contribution appears.

Several limitations define the current scope. First, the experiments use two related synthetic tasks, one predefined filter family, and small CNNs; they do not establish natural-image, large-CNN, vision-transformer, or language-model generalization. Second, architecture comparisons are not performance-matched. The lower accuracy of some GAP variants, visible in \cref{tab:endpoint-context}, means that architecture-dependent patching effects characterize the trained systems in the grid rather than an architecture effect at fixed predictive competence.

Third, patching uses complete post-ReLU feature maps from valid matched counterfactual inputs, but the resulting hybrid activation state can still be off the model's training manifold. Channel ranking is fixed from validation data using global-max activation for every architecture; random-channel analyses show that architecture dependence remains without this ranking, but the ranking changes effect magnitude. Fourth, metric choice matters. The historical normalized probability score uses a treatment-dependent denominator, so the later analyses report centered-logit fidelity and unnormalized probability error reduction separately rather than treating one score as canonical. The equivalence margins in the spatial-control study were fixed before those outcomes were inspected, but they are study-specific conventions rather than externally validated thresholds of scientific importance.

Finally, the studies were sequential. The architecture study was motivated by the intervention-size and metric results, and the spatial-control subset was chosen after the architecture study. Each fresh-block follow-up fixed its procedure before evaluating its own new outcomes, but the sequence should not be described as a single preregistered experiment. The additive reconstruction is more explicitly post-hoc: it uses already trained checkpoints and was specified after the source outcomes were known. Its role is explanatory decomposition, not independent confirmation. Neither filter similarity, visual resemblance, nor patching alone establishes semantic purity, a unique mechanism, or an identifiable causal abstraction \citep{meloux_2025_mechanistic-identifiability,sutter_2025_causal-abstraction-limits}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Preserving a predefined first-layer filter bank and measuring the effect of intervening on its activation channels answer different questions. In these controlled CNNs, the release-versus-retention patching comparison changes with intervention size, varies strongly across downstream architectures, and is affected by the original two-dimensional arrangement of the filter bank. The magnitude and interpretation of these effects also depend on the evaluation metric and channel-selection rule. Structured filters should therefore be reported as structural evidence, while activation-patching results should be reported together with the intervention size, architecture, metric, source/target construction, and channel-selection procedure that define the measurement.
