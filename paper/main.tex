\begin{abstract}
First-layer convolutional filters can remain close to predefined templates after training, but filter-template similarity does not determine how intervening on their activations changes the model output. We study this distinction in two controlled synthetic image tasks and two small CNNs. The first layer is initialized from a fixed template bank and trained either with constant regularization toward that bank or with the same regularization annealed to zero. Constant regularization yields much higher filter-template similarity. The difference in selected-channel patching score between annealed and constant regularization, however, depends strongly on the number of patched channels. In a prespecified confirmatory experiment with 20 new paired replicates of \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, the contrast comparing $k=4,8$ with $k=1,2$ is $B=0.33098$ (95\% CI $[0.27368,0.38827]$, $p=2.28\times10^{-10}$). Annealed regularization gives lower patching scores at $k=1,2$, a slightly lower score at $k=4$, and a slightly higher score at $k=8$. A separate 1,200-model sensitivity study over 50 new paired replicates gives positive values of the same contrast for TinyCNN on both tasks and across several regularization schedules, whereas TwoLayerCNN gives different, often negative estimates. An earlier positive selected-minus-random score at $k=4$ is partly explained by a decrease in the random-channel patching score, showing that the conclusion can depend on the control baseline. An independent reimplementation check on 32 checkpoints agrees with the stored results within all prespecified tolerances. Within the tested models, filter-template similarity and activation-patching results therefore capture different properties, and the effect of the regularization schedule depends on the number of patched channels and on architecture. The experiments do not establish a unique mechanism, human interpretability, or natural-image generalization.\footnote{ChatGPT (OpenAI) was used as an assistive tool during experimental planning, code development and debugging, manuscript organization, and language editing. All experimental protocols, analyses, interpretations, and manuscript text were reviewed by the authors, who take full responsibility for the work.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

A first-layer convolutional filter may remain close to a predefined edge, corner, or ring template after training. This weight-space similarity does not by itself determine how replacing the corresponding activations changes the model output. We ask whether keeping first-layer filters close to predefined templates leads to consistent activation-patching effects across different numbers of patched channels. We treat activation patching as an intervention-based measurement and do not interpret it as identifying a unique causal mechanism.

The first convolution is initialized from a fixed bank of edge, corner, and ring templates. An $L_2$ penalty toward the initial template weights can be kept constant or annealed to zero during training. Two controlled rendering tasks provide matched counterfactual image pairs, allowing filter-template similarity, predictive performance, concept selectivity and localization, and activation-patching effects to be evaluated separately.

Constant template regularization produces substantially higher filter-template similarity. The activation-patching result is more sensitive to intervention size. In \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, annealed regularization gives much lower patching scores than constant regularization when one or two channels are patched, nearly the same score at four channels, and a slightly higher score at eight. A prespecified contrast comparing these larger and smaller channel sets is positive on new data. A separate 1,200-model study reproduces a positive contrast for TinyCNN across both tasks and several regularization schedules, while TwoLayerCNN does not show the same direction. The effect of the regularization schedule on activation patching therefore changes with the number of patched channels and with architecture.

The direct selected-channel analysis was motivated by an exploratory selected-minus-random score,
\[
U(k)=F_{\mathrm{selected}}(k)-F_{\mathrm{control}}(k).
\]
At four patched channels, this score appeared to favor annealed regularization in TinyCNN. Decomposing the score showed that the positive difference was driven in substantial part by a decrease in the random-channel baseline rather than by an increase in the selected-channel patching score. We therefore prespecified the Stage-D primary contrast using the selected-channel score alone.

The study combines a controlled comparison of template initialization, constant template regularization, and annealed template regularization with a prespecified confirmatory test of sensitivity to the number of patched channels and a larger secondary sensitivity study across tasks, architectures, schedules, and all channel counts. An independent checkpoint reimplementation provides a separate check of the main stored measurements. The claims are restricted to the tested synthetic renderer, first-layer interventions, and small CNN family.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Predefined and structured convolutional filters}

Predefined spatial filters have a long history in computer vision. Gabor filters have been used to initialize the first convolutional layer \citep{ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization}, while other work maintains Gabor filter structure during training or parameterizes the first layer directly as learnable Gabor kernels \citep{molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor}. Linse et al. study networks built from fixed predefined spatial filters, and related methods incorporate predefined image operators into CNNs \citep{ma_2020_predefined-filters,linse_2024_predefined-filters}. Orozco-Solis et al. analyze the degeneration of learnable Gabor filters during training using filter-similarity metrics \citep{orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. Our setting uses a generic bank of predefined filters for initialization together with a soft regularizer toward that bank, and asks how this training constraint relates to activation-patching measurements.

Fixed spatial filters can also support strong predictors when later layers are trained to combine their outputs \citep{gaudio_2023_fixed-filters,gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions}. Accordingly, poor short-horizon performance with frozen or constrained first-layer filters does not by itself show that those features lack task-relevant information. We therefore use a linear probe on frozen features and also train the end-to-end models for longer.

\subsection{Concept-based measurements and activation patching}

Network Dissection and related work measure unit--concept correspondence \citep{bau_2017_network-dissection}, while interventions on concept-related units ask whether those units influence model behavior \citep{bau_2020_causal-units}. Concept Whitening, concept bottleneck models, and concept embedding models impose stronger architectural or supervision assumptions to learn concept-aligned representations \citep{chen_2020_concept-whitening,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks,zarlenga_2022_concept-embeddings}. TCAV instead measures a model's sensitivity to a concept direction in activation space \citep{kim_2018_tcav}. Our filter-template similarity score is a weight-space measurement and should not be conflated with these activation-space notions.

Activation patching identifies important model components by replacing their activations and measuring the resulting change in model output. Prior work shows that patching results can depend strongly on the corruption or replacement scheme, the evaluation metric, the components being patched, and the intervention granularity \citep{zhang_2024_activation-patching,heimersheim_2024_activation-patching,miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. Recent work likewise distinguishes whether information is detectable in a representation from whether it functionally influences model outputs \citep{nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. Our contribution is narrower: under a controlled template-regularization intervention, the annealed-versus-constant difference in selected-channel patching score changes systematically with the number of patched channels and is reproduced in a prespecified experiment on new replicates.

Formal work on causal abstraction and mechanistic identifiability imposes stronger requirements than our experiment satisfies \citep{geiger_2025_causal-abstraction,meloux_2025_mechanistic-identifiability,sutter_2025_causal-abstraction-limits}. Replacing first-layer activations does not identify a unique mechanism. We therefore report the intervention as a patching score tied to a specific procedure and output metric, rather than as a general measure of causal identifiability.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Tasks, models, and template bank}\label{sec:tasks-models}

Images are $32\times32$ grayscale procedural renders with balanced labels and matched nuisance contexts. Each paired replicate contains 512 training, 256 validation, and 256 test images. Rendering varies angle, line width, scale, translation, Gaussian noise, and occasional occlusion. Matched counterfactual images preserve nuisance context while changing the relevant identity or concept.

\texttt{single\_shape} classifies line, circle, triangle, and square. \texttt{two\_concepts} contains two objects and uses the presence of circle and triangle as two binary factors defining four classes. The latter therefore supplies matched one-bit concept changes while keeping the other concept fixed.

\textbf{TinyCNN} uses a $1\to16$ convolution with $9\times9$ filters, ReLU, global max pooling, and a four-class linear head. \textbf{TwoLayerCNN} adds a trainable $16\to16$ $3\times3$ convolution and ReLU before pooling. The comparison therefore probes a small increase in depth, not broad architecture generalization.

The first-layer template bank contains 16 centered, unit-norm filters: eight edges, four two-ray corners, and four rings. The corrected bank has rank 10 because some edge directions remain redundant. For a learned first-layer filter $W_i$ and template $T_j$, we summarize filter-template similarity by the average best-match signed centered cosine,
\[
A=\frac{1}{16}\sum_i\max_j\langle \widehat W_i,\widehat T_j\rangle.
\]
A separate exploratory maximum-weight one-to-one assignment analysis prevents multiple learned filters from matching to the same template. It is reported alongside the nearest-template score and does not change the activation-patching conclusions.

\subsection{Template initialization and regularization}\label{sec:retention}

All template-based conditions initialize the first convolution from the same template bank. To encourage the learned filters to remain close to those initial templates, we add the following regularization term to the training loss:
\[
\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}+
\lambda(e)\frac{\lVert W-W_{\mathrm{anchor}}\rVert_F^2}
{\lVert W_{\mathrm{anchor}}\rVert_F^2},
\]
where the penalty applies only to the first convolution and $W_{\mathrm{anchor}}$ is the initial template bank. Adam uses learning rate 0.003 and batch size 128.

The central conditions are \emph{template initialization only} (\texttt{template\_init}), with $\lambda(e)=0$ after initialization; \emph{constant template regularization} (\texttt{template\_retention\_1}), with $\lambda(e)=1$ throughout training; and \emph{annealed template regularization} (\texttt{template\_release}), with $\lambda=1$ through epoch 10, linear decay to zero by epoch 80, and zero thereafter. Stage E additionally includes constant $\lambda=0.1$ regularization and earlier or later annealing schedules.

\subsection{Concept and intervention measurements}\label{sec:measurements}

Concept AUROC quantifies how well a first-layer channel discriminates the target concept on held-out test data; the channel is chosen using validation data. Localization IoU measures the overlap between a validation-selected first-layer activation mask and the renderer's visible-object mask. These activation-space measurements are evaluated separately from filter-template similarity.

For activation patching, we use matched counterfactual image pairs. For each pair we run the model on the base image, on the matched counterfactual image, and on a patched base image in which the selected first-layer activation maps are replaced by the corresponding maps from the counterfactual run. The downstream network is otherwise unchanged. Let $p_0$ be the base output probabilities, $p_1$ the counterfactual output probabilities, and $p_S$ the patched-run probabilities. We quantify the patching effect with the normalized output-restoration score
\[
F(S)=1-\frac{\sum\lVert p_S-p_1\rVert_2^2}
{\sum\lVert p_1-p_0\rVert_2^2}.
\]
When defined, $F=0$ for a no-op intervention and $F=1$ when all first-layer channels are patched. Values below zero are possible when the patched output moves farther from the counterfactual output than the unpatched base output. This score is specific to the stated output metric and should not be interpreted as a mediated fraction.

Three channel-selection rules use validation data only: (i) standardized positive--negative activation contrast; (ii) absolute deviation of concept AUROC from 0.5; and (iii) single-channel validation patching score. We also report the accuracy of the patched run on the matched counterfactual target.

The original selected-minus-random patching score is
\[
U_{\mathrm{random}}(k)=F_{\mathrm{selected}}(k)-F_{\mathrm{random}}(k),
\]
where the random baseline averages eight deterministic channel sets of the same size. A secondary control instead selects same-size channel sets whose validation replacement energy is closest to that of the selected set, yielding $U_{\mathrm{energy}}$. Because this matching can be poor for single-channel interventions, we always report the matching error and do not use this baseline to define the primary result.

\subsection{Stage D: prospective primary estimand}\label{sec:stage-d-method}

Stage D trains only \texttt{template\_retention\_1} and the default release schedule on \texttt{two\_concepts}, for both architectures, over 20 previously unused blocks. All models train for 200 epochs; epoch 200 is the fixed inferential checkpoint. For block $b$ and number of patched channels $k$,
\[
\Delta_b(k)=F_{b}^{\mathrm{release}}(k)-F_{b}^{\mathrm{retention}}(k).
\]
The single primary ranking is the original validation contrast ranking. The predeclared contrast is
\[
B_b=\frac{\Delta_b(4)+\Delta_b(8)}{2}
-\frac{\Delta_b(1)+\Delta_b(2)}{2}.
\]
A positive $B$ indicates that the release-minus-retention difference is larger at $k=4,8$ than at $k=1,2$. This definition does not require the individual effects at $k=4$ or $k=8$ to be positive. The primary test is a two-sided one-sample Student $t$ test of $E[B]=0$ over 20 blocks. Confirmation requires all planned blocks, positive mean $B$, a two-sided 95\% $t$ interval entirely above zero, and $p<0.05$. The four individual $\Delta(k)$ values must be reported regardless of sign. AUROC and validation-patch rankings, TwoLayerCNN, absolute counterfactual accuracy, random-control $U$, and energy-matched $U$ are all predeclared secondary analyses.

\subsection{Stage E: secondary sensitivity study}\label{sec:stage-e-method}

Stage E uses 50 previously unused blocks and crosses both tasks, both architectures, and six template-prior profiles: immediate release (\texttt{template\_init}), constant $\lambda=0.1$, constant $\lambda=1$, and early, default, and late release schedules. This yields $50\times2\times2\times6=1{,}200$ trained models. At epoch 200, selected fidelity is measured for every $k=1,\ldots,16$ under all three rankings. Energy-matched controls remain restricted to $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$.

For each non-reference profile $p$, Stage E computes the same contrast $B$ relative to \texttt{retention\_1}. Five profile-versus-reference tests are Holm-adjusted within each task/architecture/ranking family. These inferences are secondary sensitivity analyses; they do not replace or enlarge the single Stage-D primary test. Release timing is treated as a sensitivity analysis rather than as a monotone dose-response experiment.

\subsection{Reproducibility checks}\label{sec:audit}

All Stage-D and Stage-E runs retain source identities, protocol identities, design manifests, and full/no-op patching checks. In addition, a separate checkpoint audit independently reimplemented the central metric calculations for 32 fixed Stage-B checkpoint evaluations and read stored scalar results only after recomputation. The corrected audit passed every frozen tolerance: maximum discrepancies were $2.22\times10^{-16}$ for alignment, $2.39\times10^{-7}$ for selected fidelity, $1.14\times10^{-7}$ for random fidelity, $2.19\times10^{-7}$ for $U$, and zero for ordinary and counterfactual accuracy. Full-patch and no-op probability errors were zero. This validates the sampled recomputations, not unsampled checkpoints or broader scientific claims.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Template retention increases alignment without a corrected patching advantage}\label{sec:stage-a-results}

Stage A strongly increases alignment in all four primary retention-versus-initialization comparisons, but none of the four $U$ tests survives Holm correction. For example, on \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, constant retention increases alignment by 0.289 but reduces test accuracy by 6.13 percentage points at 40 epochs; the paired $\Delta U=0.0252$ has marginal 95\% interval $[-0.0306,0.0811]$ and Holm-adjusted $p=0.9995$. On \texttt{single\_shape}/TinyCNN the marginal $\Delta U$ interval is positive, but its adjusted $p=0.1633$. The Stage-A results therefore support a strong alignment effect without a Holm-corrected selected-versus-random patching effect.

A frozen-feature diagnostic shows that short-horizon prediction deficits need not imply missing predictive information. With validation-selected alternative linear heads, frozen-template TinyCNN mean test accuracy rises from 83.40\% to 99.77\% on \texttt{single\_shape} and from 52.30\% to 81.80\% on \texttt{two\_concepts}. The result diagnoses downstream readout/optimization limitations in the tested models rather than an information-theoretic ceiling.

\subsection{Releasing the prior improves compositional accuracy while reducing alignment}\label{sec:stage-b-results}

Stage B shows heterogeneous early template effects. Relative to unit-normalized random initialization, \texttt{template\_init} improves mean validation accuracy over epochs 1--10 by 3.34 percentage points (95\% interval $[1.25,5.42]$) on \texttt{single\_shape}/TwoLayerCNN, but is 6.84 points worse ($[-10.26,-3.43]$) on \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN. The data therefore do not support a uniform early-learning advantage from template initialization.

Longer optimization removes much of the shallow compositional deficit. On \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, \texttt{template\_init} increases from 97.66\% test accuracy at epoch 40 to 99.30\% at epoch 200, matching unit-normalized random initialization at the final checkpoint. Constant retention reaches 98.01\%, while release reaches 99.22\%. The release-versus-retention accuracy gain is 1.21 percentage points (95\% interval $[0.71,1.71]$), while alignment falls from 0.947 to 0.580. In TwoLayerCNN, the corresponding accuracy gain is 0.39 points ($[0.04,0.74]$) and alignment falls from 0.999 to 0.907.

\subsection{The control baseline affects the selected-versus-random fidelity difference}\label{sec:decomposition}

Stage C initially showed a sign change in \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN: release-minus-retention $U_{\mathrm{random}}$ was negative for $k=1,2$ and positive for $k=4,8$ under all three rankings. Decomposing the score changes the interpretation. Under the original contrast ranking, the exploratory release-minus-retention selected-fidelity effects were $-0.375$, $-0.163$, $-0.022$, and $+0.006$ for $k=1,2,4,8$, respectively, whereas the corresponding random-fidelity changes were $-0.040$, $-0.062$, $-0.083$, and $-0.033$. The positive $\Delta U(4)=+0.061$ therefore occurs even though selected fidelity is slightly lower under release, because the random-channel baseline decreases more strongly.

The same selected-fidelity pattern appears under AUROC and validation-patch rankings: strongly negative at small patch sizes, near zero or slightly negative at four channels, and slightly positive at eight. A validation-energy-matched baseline changes the relative score but not selected fidelity. Matching quality is good for many $k=4$ and $k=8$ settings but can be poor for singleton interventions, so the energy-matched score remains a secondary diagnostic. This decomposition motivated Stage D's use of selected fidelity, rather than $U$, as the primary outcome.

\subsection{Prospective confirmation across patch sizes}\label{sec:confirmation}

\[
\bar B=0.330976,\qquad
95\%\ \mathrm{CI}=[0.273680,0.388273],
\]
with $t_{19}=12.0905$ and two-sided $p=2.28\times10^{-10}$. The frozen confirmation rule is therefore satisfied.

\subsection{One-to-one matching preserves high kernel-template similarity under retention}\label{sec:matching-results}

The exploratory matching analysis on the 2,000 saved Stage-B checkpoint states asks whether the high retained-alignment result could be produced by many learned kernels repeatedly selecting the same nearest template. Maximum-weight one-to-one assignment prevents template reuse by allowing each template to be matched at most once. At epoch 200, one-to-one assignment similarity for constant retention ranges from 0.9468 to 0.9999 across the four task/architecture settings, with essentially zero nearest-versus-assignment gap, while release means range from 0.5743 to 0.9425. The high retention alignment therefore persists under the stricter matching rule.

\section{Interpretation}\label{sec:interpretation}

Persistent template retention strongly increases kernel-template similarity, while the release-minus-retention difference measured by activation patching varies with the number of patched channels. In the primary TinyCNN setting, release is much less favorable at one and two patched channels than at four and eight. TwoLayerCNN shows a different pattern. A single patch size therefore does not summarize the release-versus-retention comparison across the tested models.

The distinction between selected-channel fidelity and a selected-versus-control score also matters. In Stage C, the positive release-minus-retention value of $U_{\mathrm{random}}(4)$ occurs even though selected fidelity is slightly lower under release, because random-channel fidelity decreases more strongly. Reporting the selected intervention and its control separately avoids attributing a relative change to the selected channels alone.

The experiments support a measurement-level interpretation rather than a mechanistic one. Variation with patch size could reflect redundancy, nonlinear channel interactions, ranking behavior, activation scale, or patched combinations that do not occur naturally. The change in direction between TinyCNN and TwoLayerCNN provides additional evidence that the observed patching pattern should not be treated as a general property of template priors.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item Only two related synthetic tasks, two small architectures, one first-layer template bank, and one family of optimizers/schedules are studied. There is no natural-image benchmark or human-readability experiment.
\item The renderer provides balanced, matched counterfactuals and exact object masks. This control is useful for measurement but simplifies correlations and nuisance structure found in real data.
\item The confirmed Stage-D claim is specific to \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, the contrast ranking, epoch 200, and $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$. Stage-E evidence extends the sensitivity analysis but remains secondary.
\item Stage E explores six prior profiles and all $k=1,\ldots,16$. Holm adjustment is applied only within the predeclared five-profile families; the study is not a license to select arbitrary favorable subgroups.
\item Activation patching changes complete first-layer feature maps and can create internal combinations that are not on the model's natural activation manifold. Fidelity is tied to probability outputs and the chosen layer/ranking; other intervention metrics may differ \citep{zhang_2024_activation-patching,heimersheim_2024_activation-patching}.
\item $U_{\mathrm{random}}$ and $U_{\mathrm{energy}}$ are relative scores. The random baseline changes with patch size, while energy matching is imperfect, especially for singleton interventions. Neither relative score should replace the directly reported selected fidelity.
\item The one-to-one matching result concerns a redundant rank-10 template bank and does not validate human interpretability or independent semantic coverage.
\item The independent audit covers 32 fixed Stage-B checkpoint evaluations rather than every checkpoint from every stage. Passing the audit supports the sampled implementation but is not external replication.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and future work}\label{sec:future}

A mechanism-focused follow-up could measure channel redundancy, joint versus singleton effects, subspace-level interventions, activation-scale controls, and architecture-by-patch-size interactions under a predeclared analysis plan. Moving beyond the present renderer would test whether the patch-size dependence persists when concept correlations and nuisance structure are less controlled. Claims about human interpretability would additionally require direct human evaluation rather than inference from kernel-template similarity.

Natural-image experiments would address external validity, and larger architectures could help determine whether the TwoLayerCNN result reflects depth, optimization, or a property of this particular network. Alternative intervention targets and output metrics would test whether the confirmed contrast is specific to first-layer probability-space activation patching.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Persistent template retention produces high first-layer kernel-template similarity in these small CNNs. Activation patching gives a more conditional result. In the prospectively confirmed TinyCNN setting, the release-minus-retention selected-fidelity effect is strongly negative at one and two patched channels, close to zero at four, and slightly positive at eight. A separate 1,200-model study gives positive values of the same predeclared contrast for TinyCNN on both tasks, while TwoLayerCNN behaves differently. The selected-versus-random analysis also shows that a control baseline can change a relative patching comparison. Kernel-template similarity, selected-channel fidelity, and control-relative scores should therefore be reported as separate measurements, with the number of patched channels and architecture specified as part of the result.
