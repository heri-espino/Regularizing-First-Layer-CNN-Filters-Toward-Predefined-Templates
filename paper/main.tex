\begin{abstract}
We study how constraining first-layer convolutional filters toward predefined templates affects activation-patching measurements. In two controlled synthetic image tasks and two small CNNs, we initialize the first layer from a fixed template bank and either keep an $L_2$ penalty toward the initial filters throughout training or anneal that penalty to zero. Constant regularization keeps the learned filters much closer to the predefined filters, but the annealed-minus-constant difference in patching effect depends strongly on how many channels are patched. In a prespecified confirmatory experiment with 20 new replicates of \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, with conditions paired within each replicate, the contrast comparing $k=4,8$ with $k=1,2$ is $B=0.33098$ (95\% CI $[0.27368,0.38827]$, $p=2.28\times10^{-10}$). Annealed regularization gives lower patching effects at $k=1,2$, a slightly lower effect at $k=4$, and a slightly higher effect at $k=8$. In a separate 1,200-model sensitivity analysis over 50 new replicates, the same contrast is positive for TinyCNN on both tasks and across several regularization schedules, whereas TwoLayerCNN gives different, often negative estimates. An earlier positive value of the selected-minus-random difference at $k=4$ is partly due to a lower patching effect for random channels, showing that the comparison depends on the choice of baseline. An independent reimplementation of the checkpoint evaluation reproduces the original results at 32 checkpoints within all prespecified tolerances. Within the tested models, similarity to the predefined filters and activation-patching effects are therefore not interchangeable measurements, and the regularization-schedule comparison depends on both the number of patched channels and the architecture. The experiments do not establish a unique mechanism, human interpretability, or generalization to natural images.\footnote{ChatGPT (OpenAI) was used as an assistive tool during experimental planning, code development and debugging, manuscript organization, and language editing. All experimental protocols, analyses, interpretations, and manuscript text were reviewed by the authors, who take full responsibility for the work.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Predefined spatial filters provide an explicit structure for the first convolutional layer, but similarity between learned filters and those predefined filters does not by itself determine how the corresponding activations affect model output. We ask whether constraining first-layer filters toward predefined templates leads to consistent activation-patching effects as the number of patched channels changes. Activation patching is used here as an intervention on selected channels, not as evidence for a unique causal mechanism.

The first convolution is initialized from a fixed bank of edge, corner, and ring filters. An $L_2$ penalty toward the initial filter weights is either kept constant or annealed to zero during training. Two controlled rendering tasks provide matched counterfactual image pairs, allowing similarity to the predefined filters, predictive performance, concept selectivity and localization, and activation-patching effects to be evaluated separately.

Constant template regularization substantially increases filter-template similarity, but the patching comparison changes with the number of patched channels. The prespecified TinyCNN contrast comparing $k=4,8$ with $k=1,2$ is positive in 20 new replicates, and the larger sensitivity analysis shows the same direction for TinyCNN across both tasks and several regularization schedules. TwoLayerCNN shows a different pattern.

The direct analysis of selected channels was motivated by an exploratory comparison with random channels,
\[
U_{\mathrm{random}}(k)=F_{\mathrm{selected}}(k)-F_{\mathrm{random}}(k).
\]
At four patched channels, this difference appeared to favor annealed regularization in TinyCNN. Examining its two terms showed that the positive value was driven in substantial part by a lower random-channel baseline rather than by a larger patching effect for selected channels. We therefore prespecified the Stage-D primary contrast using the patching effect for selected channels alone.

We compare template initialization, constant template regularization, and annealed template regularization in a staged experimental design. A prespecified confirmatory experiment tests the channel-count contrast on new replicates, and a larger secondary analysis examines its sensitivity to task, architecture, regularization schedule, and channel count. An independent reimplementation of the checkpoint evaluation provides a separate reproducibility check.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Predefined and structured convolutional filters}

Predefined spatial filters have a long history in computer vision. Gabor filters have been used to initialize the first convolutional layer \citep{ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization}, while other work maintains Gabor filter structure during training or parameterizes the first layer directly as learnable Gabor kernels \citep{molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor}. Linse et al. study networks built from fixed predefined spatial filters, and related methods incorporate predefined image operators into CNNs \citep{ma_2020_predefined-filters,linse_2024_predefined-filters}. Orozco-Solis et al. analyze the degeneration of learnable Gabor filters during training using filter-similarity metrics \citep{orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. Our setting uses a generic bank of predefined filters for initialization together with a soft regularizer toward that bank, and asks how this training constraint relates to activation-patching measurements.

Fixed spatial filters can also support strong predictors when later layers are trained to combine their outputs \citep{gaudio_2023_fixed-filters,gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions}. Poor short-horizon performance with frozen or constrained first-layer filters therefore does not by itself show that those features lack task-relevant information. We use a linear probe on frozen features and also train the end-to-end models for longer.

\subsection{Concept-based measurements and activation patching}

Network Dissection and related work measure unit--concept correspondence \citep{bau_2017_network-dissection}, while interventions on concept-related units ask whether those units influence model behavior \citep{bau_2020_causal-units}. Concept Whitening, concept bottleneck models, and concept embedding models impose stronger architectural or supervision assumptions to learn concept-aligned representations \citep{chen_2020_concept-whitening,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks,zarlenga_2022_concept-embeddings}. TCAV instead measures a model's sensitivity to a concept direction in activation space \citep{kim_2018_tcav}. Our filter-template similarity score is a weight-space measurement and should not be conflated with these activation-space notions.

Activation patching identifies important model components by replacing their activations and measuring the resulting change in model output. Prior work shows that patching results can depend strongly on the corruption or replacement scheme, the evaluation metric, the components being patched, and the intervention granularity \citep{zhang_2024_activation-patching,heimersheim_2024_activation-patching,miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. Recent work likewise distinguishes whether information is detectable in a representation from whether it functionally influences model outputs \citep{nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. Here we ask a narrower question: under a controlled comparison of regularization schedules, how does the annealed-minus-constant patching effect change as more channels are patched? The resulting channel-count contrast is then tested in a prespecified experiment on new replicates.

Causal abstraction and mechanistic identifiability address stronger claims about internal mechanisms \citep{geiger_2025_causal-abstraction,meloux_2025_mechanistic-identifiability,sutter_2025_causal-abstraction-limits}. Replacing first-layer activations does not identify a unique mechanism, so $F$ is interpreted only for the specified patching procedure and output metric.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Tasks, models, and template bank}\label{sec:tasks-models}

Images are $32\times32$ grayscale procedural renders with balanced labels and matched nuisance variables. Each replicate contains 512 training, 256 validation, and 256 test images. Rendering varies angle, line width, scale, translation, Gaussian noise, and occasional occlusion. Matched counterfactual images preserve the nuisance variables while changing the relevant identity or concept.

\texttt{single\_shape} classifies line, circle, triangle, and square. \texttt{two\_concepts} contains two objects and uses the presence of circle and triangle as two binary factors defining four classes. For matched counterfactual pairs, one concept changes while the other is held fixed.

\textbf{TinyCNN} uses a $1\to16$ convolution with $9\times9$ filters, ReLU, global max pooling, and a four-class linear head. \textbf{TwoLayerCNN} adds a trainable $16\to16$ $3\times3$ convolution and ReLU before pooling. This comparison probes a small increase in depth rather than broad architectural generalization.

The first-layer template bank contains 16 centered, unit-norm filters: eight edges, four two-ray corners, and four rings. The bank has rank 10 because some edge directions are redundant. For a learned first-layer filter $W_i$ and template $T_j$, we summarize filter-template similarity by the average best-match signed centered cosine,
\[
A=\frac{1}{16}\sum_i\max_j\langle \widehat W_i,\widehat T_j\rangle.
\]
We also compute an exploratory maximum-weight one-to-one assignment, which matches each learned filter to a distinct template. Both matching summaries are reported.

\subsection{Template initialization and regularization}\label{sec:retention}

All template-based conditions initialize the first convolution from the same template bank. To encourage the learned filters to remain close to those initial templates, we add the following regularization term to the training loss:
\[
\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}+
\lambda(e)\frac{\lVert W-W_{\mathrm{anchor}}\rVert_F^2}
{\lVert W_{\mathrm{anchor}}\rVert_F^2},
\]
where the penalty applies only to the first convolution and $W_{\mathrm{anchor}}$ is the initial template bank. Adam uses learning rate 0.003 and batch size 128.

The central conditions are \emph{template initialization only} (\texttt{template\_init}), with $\lambda(e)=0$ after initialization; \emph{constant template regularization} (\texttt{template\_retention\_1}), with $\lambda(e)=1$ throughout training; and \emph{annealed template regularization} (\texttt{template\_release}), with $\lambda=1$ through epoch 10, linear decay to zero by epoch 80, and zero thereafter. Stage E additionally includes constant $\lambda=0.1$ regularization and earlier or later annealing schedules.

\subsection{Concept and intervention measurements}\label{sec:measurements}

Concept AUROC quantifies how well a first-layer channel discriminates the target concept on held-out test data; the channel is chosen using validation data. Localization IoU measures the overlap between a validation-selected first-layer activation mask and the renderer's visible-object mask. These activation-space measurements are evaluated separately from filter-template similarity.

For activation patching, we use matched counterfactual image pairs. For each pair we run the model on the base image, on the matched counterfactual image, and on a patched base image in which the selected first-layer activation maps are replaced by the corresponding maps from the counterfactual run. The downstream network is otherwise unchanged. Let $p_0$ be the base output probabilities, $p_1$ the counterfactual output probabilities, and $p_S$ the patched-run probabilities. We quantify the patching effect with the normalized metric
\[
F(S)=1-\frac{\sum\lVert p_S-p_1\rVert_2^2}
{\sum\lVert p_1-p_0\rVert_2^2}.
\]
When defined, $F=0$ for a no-op intervention and $F=1$ when all first-layer channels are patched. Values below zero are possible when the patched output moves farther from the counterfactual output than the unpatched base output. This metric is tied to the stated output distance and is not interpreted as a mediated fraction.

Three channel-selection rules use validation data only: (i) the standardized difference in mean activation between concept-positive and concept-negative examples; (ii) absolute deviation of concept AUROC from 0.5; and (iii) the single-channel validation patching effect. We also report the accuracy of the patched run on the matched counterfactual target.

For the original random-channel comparison, we report
\[
U_{\mathrm{random}}(k)=F_{\mathrm{selected}}(k)-F_{\mathrm{random}}(k),
\]
where the random baseline averages eight deterministic channel sets of the same size. Thus, $U_{\mathrm{random}}$ is the difference in patching effect between the selected channels and random channel sets. A secondary control instead selects same-size channel sets whose validation replacement energy is closest to that of the selected set, yielding $U_{\mathrm{energy}}$. Because this matching can be poor for single-channel interventions, we always report the matching error and do not use this baseline to define the primary result.

\subsection{Stage D: prespecified confirmatory test}\label{sec:stage-d-method}

Stage D compares constant template regularization (\texttt{template\_retention\_1}) with the default annealing schedule (\texttt{template\_release}) on \texttt{two\_concepts} for both architectures over 20 new replicates, pairing the two conditions within each replicate. All models train for 200 epochs, with epoch 200 fixed as the evaluation point. For replicate $b$ and number of patched channels $k$,
\[
\Delta_b(k)=F_{b}^{\mathrm{annealed}}(k)-F_{b}^{\mathrm{constant}}(k).
\]
For the primary analysis, channels are ranked by the standardized mean activation difference on the validation set. The prespecified contrast is
\[
B_b=\frac{\Delta_b(4)+\Delta_b(8)}{2}
-\frac{\Delta_b(1)+\Delta_b(2)}{2}.
\]
A positive $B$ indicates that the annealed-minus-constant difference is larger at $k=4,8$ than at $k=1,2$; it does not require the differences at $k=4$ or $k=8$ themselves to be positive. The primary test is a two-sided one-sample Student $t$ test of $E[B]=0$ across the 20 replicate-level contrasts. The prespecified criterion for confirmation requires all planned replicates, a positive mean $B$, a two-sided 95\% $t$ interval entirely above zero, and $p<0.05$. The four individual $\Delta(k)$ values are reported regardless of sign. The two alternative channel-selection rules, TwoLayerCNN, patched-run accuracy, random-channel $U$, and energy-matched $U$ are all prespecified secondary analyses.

\subsection{Stage E: secondary sensitivity analysis}\label{sec:stage-e-method}

Stage E uses 50 new replicates across both tasks, both architectures, and six template-regularization schedules: initialization only (\texttt{template\_init}), constant $\lambda=0.1$, constant $\lambda=1$, and early, default, and late annealing schedules. This yields $50\times2\times2\times6=1{,}200$ trained models. At epoch 200, the patching effect for selected channels is measured for every $k=1,\ldots,16$ under all three channel-selection rules. Energy-matched controls remain restricted to $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$.

For each non-reference schedule $p$, Stage E computes the same contrast $B$ relative to constant $\lambda=1$ regularization. Five schedule-versus-reference tests are Holm-adjusted within each task/architecture/channel-selection family. These tests are secondary to the single Stage-D primary analysis. Because the schedules change both the timing and duration of regularization, they are treated as sensitivity analyses rather than as a single ordered regularization factor.

\subsection{Reproducibility checks}\label{sec:audit}

All Stage-D and Stage-E runs retain source hashes, protocol hashes, run manifests, and full-channel/no-op patching sanity checks. An independent implementation also recomputed the main metrics for 32 fixed Stage-B checkpoints without reading the stored scalar outputs beforehand. After correction of an implementation error described in Appendix~\ref{app:audit}, every recomputed value was within its prespecified tolerance. The maximum discrepancies were $2.22\times10^{-16}$ for filter-template similarity, $2.39\times10^{-7}$ for the patching effect for selected channels, $1.14\times10^{-7}$ for the patching effect for random channels, $2.19\times10^{-7}$ for $U$, and zero for ordinary accuracy and patched-run accuracy on counterfactual targets. Full-channel and no-op probability errors were zero. This is a reproducibility check on fixed checkpoints, not an external replication.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Constant template regularization increases filter-template similarity, but not the Holm-corrected selected-minus-random comparison}\label{sec:stage-a-results}

In Stage A, constant regularization increases filter-template similarity in all four comparisons with initialization only, but none of the four selected-minus-random $U$ tests survives Holm correction. For example, on \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, constant regularization increases filter-template similarity by 0.289 but reduces test accuracy by 6.13 percentage points at 40 epochs; the paired $\Delta U=0.0252$ has marginal 95\% interval $[-0.0306,0.0811]$ and Holm-adjusted $p=0.9995$. On \texttt{single\_shape}/TinyCNN the marginal $\Delta U$ interval is positive, but its adjusted $p=0.1633$. Thus, the clear change in filter-template similarity is not accompanied by a Holm-corrected change in the selected-minus-random patching comparison.

A linear-probe analysis on frozen first-layer features shows that poor short-horizon accuracy need not imply that the features lack task-relevant information. With validation-selected alternative linear heads, mean TinyCNN test accuracy rises from 83.40\% to 99.77\% on \texttt{single\_shape} and from 52.30\% to 81.80\% on \texttt{two\_concepts}. This suggests that downstream readout and optimization contribute to the early accuracy gap.

\subsection{Annealing template regularization improves two-concept accuracy while reducing filter-template similarity}\label{sec:stage-b-results}

Stage B shows heterogeneous early effects of template initialization. Relative to unit-normalized random initialization, \texttt{template\_init} improves mean validation accuracy over epochs 1--10 by 3.34 percentage points (95\% interval $[1.25,5.42]$) on \texttt{single\_shape}/TwoLayerCNN, but is 6.84 points worse ($[-10.26,-3.43]$) on \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN. Thus, template initialization does not provide a consistent early optimization advantage across the tested settings.

Longer training removes much of the early TinyCNN accuracy gap on \texttt{two\_concepts}. The initialization-only condition rises from 97.66\% test accuracy at epoch 40 to 99.30\% at epoch 200, matching unit-normalized random initialization at the final checkpoint. Constant template regularization reaches 98.01\%, while annealed regularization reaches 99.22\%. The annealed-minus-constant accuracy difference is 1.21 percentage points (95\% interval $[0.71,1.71]$), while filter-template similarity decreases from 0.947 to 0.580. In TwoLayerCNN, the corresponding accuracy difference is 0.39 points ($[0.04,0.74]$), while filter-template similarity decreases from 0.999 to 0.907.

In this setting, constant regularization keeps the first-layer filters close to the templates, whereas annealing the penalty allows them to drift farther while slightly improving test accuracy on \texttt{two\_concepts}. The experiment does not establish that reduced filter-template similarity causes the accuracy improvement.

\subsection{The random-channel baseline changes the selected-minus-random comparison}\label{sec:decomposition}

Stage C initially showed a sign change in \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN: the annealed-minus-constant difference in $U_{\mathrm{random}}$ was negative for $k=1,2$ and positive for $k=4,8$ under all three channel-selection rules. The two terms of $U$ show why the sign changes. When channels are ranked by activation difference, the exploratory differences in patching effect for selected channels were $-0.375$, $-0.163$, $-0.022$, and $+0.006$ for $k=1,2,4,8$, respectively, whereas the corresponding random-channel differences were $-0.040$, $-0.062$, $-0.083$, and $-0.033$. Thus, $\Delta U(4)=+0.061$ even though the patching effect for selected channels is slightly lower with annealed regularization, because the patching effect for random channels decreases more strongly.

The same pattern in the selected-channel effect appears under the AUROC and validation-patching channel-selection rules: strongly negative at one or two channels, near zero or slightly negative at four, and slightly positive at eight. An energy-matched control changes the selected-minus-control difference in patching effect but not the patching effect for selected channels itself. Matching is generally tight for $k=4$ and $k=8$ but can be poor for single-channel interventions, so the energy-matched analysis remains secondary. This decomposition motivated the use of the patching effect for selected channels, rather than $U$, as the Stage-D primary outcome.

95\%\ \mathrm{CI}=[0.273680,0.388273],

\subsection{One-to-one matching gives similar filter-template similarity under constant regularization}\label{sec:matching-results}

To check whether high nearest-template similarity could be driven by repeated matches to the same few templates, we reanalyze the 2,000 saved Stage-B checkpoint states using maximum-weight one-to-one assignment. This matching assigns each learned filter to a distinct template. At epoch 200, one-to-one similarity under constant regularization ranges from 0.9468 to 0.9999 across the four task/architecture settings, with essentially no difference from the nearest-template score, whereas the annealed-regularization means range from 0.5743 to 0.9425. The high similarity under constant regularization is therefore similar under the stricter one-to-one matching rule.

\section{Interpretation}\label{sec:interpretation}

Constant template regularization strongly increases filter-template similarity, while the annealed-minus-constant difference in the normalized patching effect varies with the number of patched channels. In the primary TinyCNN setting, the annealed-minus-constant difference is much more negative at one and two patched channels than at four and eight. TwoLayerCNN shows a different pattern. A result at a single channel count therefore does not summarize the regularization-schedule comparison across the tested models.

The distinction between the patching effect for selected channels and a comparison against a control baseline also matters. In Stage C, the positive annealed-minus-constant value of $U_{\mathrm{random}}(4)$ occurs even though the patching effect for selected channels is slightly lower under annealed regularization, because the patching effect for random channels decreases more strongly. Reporting the selected-channel and control-channel effects separately avoids attributing a relative difference to the selected channels alone.

These results show that the measured patching effect is sensitive to how many channels are intervened on; they do not identify a unique mechanism. Variation with the number of patched channels could reflect redundancy, nonlinear channel interactions, the channel-selection rule, activation scale, or out-of-distribution combinations of activations. The different pattern in TwoLayerCNN also argues against treating this as a general property of template-based regularization.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item Only two related synthetic tasks, two small architectures, one first-layer template bank, one optimizer, and one family of regularization schedules are studied. There is no natural-image benchmark or human-interpretability experiment.
\item The renderer provides balanced, matched counterfactuals and exact object masks. This control is useful for measurement but simplifies correlations and nuisance structure found in real data.
\item The confirmed Stage-D result is specific to \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, ranking channels by activation difference, epoch 200, and $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$. Stage-E evidence broadens the sensitivity analysis but remains secondary.
\item Stage E explores six regularization schedules and all $k=1,\ldots,16$. Holm adjustment is applied only within the prespecified five-comparison families; these analyses should not be used to select favorable post hoc subgroups.
\item Activation patching replaces complete first-layer feature maps and can create activation combinations that are out of distribution for the model. The normalized patching effect is tied to probability outputs, the chosen layer, and the channel-selection rule; other intervention metrics may give different results \citep{zhang_2024_activation-patching,heimersheim_2024_activation-patching}.
\item $U_{\mathrm{random}}$ and $U_{\mathrm{energy}}$ are selected-minus-control differences in patching effect. The random baseline changes with the number of patched channels, while energy matching is imperfect, especially for single-channel interventions. Neither score should replace the directly reported patching effect for selected channels.
\item The one-to-one matching analysis concerns a redundant rank-10 template bank and does not show that every filter has a distinct semantic meaning or that the filters are human-interpretable.
\item The independent reimplementation covers 32 fixed Stage-B checkpoints rather than every checkpoint from every stage. Agreement on these checkpoints supports the reproducibility of the evaluation code but is not an external replication.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and future work}\label{sec:future}

Future experiments could test channel redundancy, joint versus single-channel effects, subspace-level interventions, activation-scale controls, and architecture-by-channel-count interactions under a prespecified analysis plan. Natural-image data and larger architectures would test whether the channel-count dependence persists beyond the present renderer and whether the TwoLayerCNN result reflects depth, optimization, or this particular architecture. Alternative intervention targets and evaluation metrics would test how specific the confirmed contrast is to first-layer patching with the present probability-based metric. Claims about human interpretability would require direct human evaluation rather than inference from filter-template similarity.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Constant template regularization produces high first-layer filter-template similarity in these small CNNs. The activation-patching comparison is more conditional. In the prespecified TinyCNN confirmation, the annealed-minus-constant difference in patching effect for selected channels is strongly negative at one and two patched channels, close to zero at four, and slightly positive at eight. A separate 1,200-model sensitivity analysis gives positive values of the same prespecified channel-count contrast for TinyCNN on both tasks, while TwoLayerCNN behaves differently. The selected-minus-random analysis further shows that a relative patching comparison can change with the control baseline. Similarity to the predefined filters, the patching effect for selected channels, and the corresponding control-channel effect should therefore be reported separately, together with the number of patched channels and the architecture.
