\begin{abstract}
First-layer convolutional filters can remain close to predefined templates after training, but filter-template similarity does not determine how intervening on their activations changes the model output. We study this distinction in two controlled synthetic image tasks and two small CNNs. The first layer is initialized from a fixed template bank and trained either with constant regularization toward that bank or with the same regularization annealed to zero. Constant regularization yields much higher filter-template similarity. The difference in selected-channel patching score between annealed and constant regularization, however, depends strongly on the number of patched channels. In a prespecified confirmatory experiment with 20 new paired replicates of \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, the contrast comparing $k=4,8$ with $k=1,2$ is $B=0.33098$ (95\% CI $[0.27368,0.38827]$, $p=2.28\times10^{-10}$). Annealed regularization gives lower patching scores at $k=1,2$, a slightly lower score at $k=4$, and a slightly higher score at $k=8$. A separate 1,200-model sensitivity study over 50 new paired replicates gives positive values of the same contrast for TinyCNN on both tasks and across several regularization schedules, whereas TwoLayerCNN gives different, often negative estimates. An earlier positive selected-minus-random score at $k=4$ is partly explained by a decrease in the random-channel patching score, showing that the conclusion can depend on the control baseline. An independent reimplementation check on 32 checkpoints agrees with the stored results within all prespecified tolerances. Within the tested models, filter-template similarity and activation-patching results therefore capture different properties, and the effect of the regularization schedule depends on the number of patched channels and on architecture. The experiments do not establish a unique mechanism, human interpretability, or natural-image generalization.\footnote{ChatGPT (OpenAI) was used as an assistive tool during experimental planning, code development and debugging, manuscript organization, and language editing. All experimental protocols, analyses, interpretations, and manuscript text were reviewed by the authors, who take full responsibility for the work.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

A first-layer convolutional filter may remain close to a predefined edge, corner, or ring template after training. This weight-space similarity does not by itself determine how replacing the corresponding activations changes the model output. We ask whether keeping first-layer filters close to predefined templates leads to consistent activation-patching effects across different numbers of patched channels. We use activation patching as a functional test of the selected channels, not as evidence that they form a unique causal mechanism.

The first convolution is initialized from a fixed bank of edge, corner, and ring templates. An $L_2$ penalty toward the initial template weights can be kept constant or annealed to zero during training. Two controlled rendering tasks provide matched counterfactual image pairs, allowing filter-template similarity, predictive performance, concept selectivity and localization, and activation-patching effects to be evaluated separately.

Constant template regularization produces substantially higher filter-template similarity. The patching comparison, however, changes with the number of patched channels. In \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, annealed regularization gives much lower patching scores than constant regularization when one or two channels are patched, nearly the same score at four channels, and a slightly higher score at eight. A prespecified contrast comparing these larger and smaller channel sets is positive on new data. A separate 1,200-model study reproduces a positive contrast for TinyCNN across both tasks and several regularization schedules, while TwoLayerCNN does not show the same direction. The effect of the regularization schedule on activation patching therefore changes with the number of patched channels and with architecture.

The direct selected-channel analysis was motivated by an exploratory selected-minus-random score,
\[
U(k)=F_{\mathrm{selected}}(k)-F_{\mathrm{control}}(k).
\]
At four patched channels, this score appeared to favor annealed regularization in TinyCNN. Decomposing the score showed that the positive difference was driven in substantial part by a decrease in the random-channel baseline rather than by an increase in the selected-channel patching score. We therefore prespecified the Stage-D primary contrast using the selected-channel score alone.

The study combines a controlled comparison of template initialization, constant template regularization, and annealed template regularization with a prespecified confirmatory test of sensitivity to the number of patched channels and a larger secondary sensitivity study across tasks, architectures, schedules, and all channel counts. An independent checkpoint reimplementation provides a separate check of the main stored measurements. The claims are restricted to the tested synthetic renderer, first-layer interventions, and small CNN family.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Predefined and structured convolutional filters}

Predefined spatial filters have a long history in computer vision. Gabor filters have been used to initialize the first convolutional layer \citep{ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization}, while other work maintains Gabor filter structure during training or parameterizes the first layer directly as learnable Gabor kernels \citep{molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor}. Linse et al. study networks built from fixed predefined spatial filters, and related methods incorporate predefined image operators into CNNs \citep{ma_2020_predefined-filters,linse_2024_predefined-filters}. Orozco-Solis et al. analyze the degeneration of learnable Gabor filters during training using filter-similarity metrics \citep{orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. Our setting uses a generic bank of predefined filters for initialization together with a soft regularizer toward that bank, and asks how this training constraint relates to activation-patching measurements.

Fixed spatial filters can also support strong predictors when later layers are trained to combine their outputs \citep{gaudio_2023_fixed-filters,gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions}. Accordingly, poor short-horizon performance with frozen or constrained first-layer filters does not by itself show that those features lack task-relevant information. We therefore use a linear probe on frozen features and also train the end-to-end models for longer.

\subsection{Concept-based measurements and activation patching}

Network Dissection and related work measure unit--concept correspondence \citep{bau_2017_network-dissection}, while interventions on concept-related units ask whether those units influence model behavior \citep{bau_2020_causal-units}. Concept Whitening, concept bottleneck models, and concept embedding models impose stronger architectural or supervision assumptions to learn concept-aligned representations \citep{chen_2020_concept-whitening,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks,zarlenga_2022_concept-embeddings}. TCAV instead measures a model's sensitivity to a concept direction in activation space \citep{kim_2018_tcav}. Our filter-template similarity score is a weight-space measurement and should not be conflated with these activation-space notions.

Activation patching identifies important model components by replacing their activations and measuring the resulting change in model output. Prior work shows that patching results can depend strongly on the corruption or replacement scheme, the evaluation metric, the components being patched, and the intervention granularity \citep{zhang_2024_activation-patching,heimersheim_2024_activation-patching,miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. Recent work likewise distinguishes whether information is detectable in a representation from whether it functionally influences model outputs \citep{nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. Our contribution is narrower: under a controlled comparison of template-regularization schedules, the annealed-versus-constant difference in selected-channel patching score changes systematically with the number of patched channels and is reproduced in a prespecified experiment on new replicates.

Formal work on causal abstraction and mechanistic identifiability imposes stronger requirements than our experiment satisfies \citep{geiger_2025_causal-abstraction,meloux_2025_mechanistic-identifiability,sutter_2025_causal-abstraction-limits}. Replacing first-layer activations does not identify a unique mechanism. Accordingly, we interpret $F$ only as a score for the specified patching procedure and output metric, not as a general measure of causal identifiability.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Tasks, models, and template bank}\label{sec:tasks-models}

Images are $32\times32$ grayscale procedural renders with balanced labels and matched nuisance contexts. Each paired replicate contains 512 training, 256 validation, and 256 test images. Rendering varies angle, line width, scale, translation, Gaussian noise, and occasional occlusion. Matched counterfactual images preserve nuisance context while changing the relevant identity or concept.

\texttt{single\_shape} classifies line, circle, triangle, and square. \texttt{two\_concepts} contains two objects and uses the presence of circle and triangle as two binary factors defining four classes. For matched counterfactual pairs, one concept changes while the other is held fixed.

\textbf{TinyCNN} uses a $1\to16$ convolution with $9\times9$ filters, ReLU, global max pooling, and a four-class linear head. \textbf{TwoLayerCNN} adds a trainable $16\to16$ $3\times3$ convolution and ReLU before pooling. The comparison therefore probes a small increase in depth, not broad architecture generalization.

The first-layer template bank contains 16 centered, unit-norm filters: eight edges, four two-ray corners, and four rings. The corrected bank has rank 10 because some edge directions remain redundant. For a learned first-layer filter $W_i$ and template $T_j$, we summarize filter-template similarity by the average best-match signed centered cosine,
\[
A=\frac{1}{16}\sum_i\max_j\langle \widehat W_i,\widehat T_j\rangle.
\]
A separate exploratory maximum-weight one-to-one assignment analysis prevents multiple learned filters from matching to the same template. It is reported alongside the nearest-template score and does not change the activation-patching conclusions.

\subsection{Template initialization and regularization}\label{sec:retention}

All template-based conditions initialize the first convolution from the same template bank. To encourage the learned filters to remain close to those initial templates, we add the following regularization term to the training loss:
\[
\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}+
\lambda(e)\frac{\lVert W-W_{\mathrm{anchor}}\rVert_F^2}
{\lVert W_{\mathrm{anchor}}\rVert_F^2},
\]
where the penalty applies only to the first convolution and $W_{\mathrm{anchor}}$ is the initial template bank. Adam uses learning rate 0.003 and batch size 128.

The central conditions are \emph{template initialization only} (\texttt{template\_init}), with $\lambda(e)=0$ after initialization; \emph{constant template regularization} (\texttt{template\_retention\_1}), with $\lambda(e)=1$ throughout training; and \emph{annealed template regularization} (\texttt{template\_release}), with $\lambda=1$ through epoch 10, linear decay to zero by epoch 80, and zero thereafter. Stage E additionally includes constant $\lambda=0.1$ regularization and earlier or later annealing schedules.

\subsection{Concept and intervention measurements}\label{sec:measurements}

Concept AUROC quantifies how well a first-layer channel discriminates the target concept on held-out test data; the channel is chosen using validation data. Localization IoU measures the overlap between a validation-selected first-layer activation mask and the renderer's visible-object mask. These activation-space measurements are evaluated separately from filter-template similarity.

For activation patching, we use matched counterfactual image pairs. For each pair we run the model on the base image, on the matched counterfactual image, and on a patched base image in which the selected first-layer activation maps are replaced by the corresponding maps from the counterfactual run. The downstream network is otherwise unchanged. Let $p_0$ be the base output probabilities, $p_1$ the counterfactual output probabilities, and $p_S$ the patched-run probabilities. We summarize the patching effect with the normalized score
\[
F(S)=1-\frac{\sum\lVert p_S-p_1\rVert_2^2}
{\sum\lVert p_1-p_0\rVert_2^2}.
\]
When defined, $F=0$ for a no-op intervention and $F=1$ when all first-layer channels are patched. Values below zero are possible when the patched output moves farther from the counterfactual output than the unpatched base output. This score is specific to the stated output metric and should not be interpreted as a mediated fraction.

Three channel-selection rules use validation data only: (i) the standardized difference in mean activation between concept-positive and concept-negative examples; (ii) absolute deviation of concept AUROC from 0.5; and (iii) the single-channel validation patching score. We also report the accuracy of the patched run on the matched counterfactual target.

The original selected-minus-random patching score is
\[
U_{\mathrm{random}}(k)=F_{\mathrm{selected}}(k)-F_{\mathrm{random}}(k),
\]
where the random baseline averages eight deterministic channel sets of the same size. A secondary control instead selects same-size channel sets whose validation replacement energy is closest to that of the selected set, yielding $U_{\mathrm{energy}}$. Because this matching can be poor for single-channel interventions, we always report the matching error and do not use this baseline to define the primary result.

\subsection{Stage D: prespecified confirmatory test}\label{sec:stage-d-method}

Stage D compares constant template regularization (\texttt{template\_retention\_1}) with the default annealing schedule (\texttt{template\_release}) on \texttt{two\_concepts}, for both architectures, over 20 new paired replicates. All models train for 200 epochs, with epoch 200 fixed as the evaluation point. For replicate $b$ and number of patched channels $k$,
\[
\Delta_b(k)=F_{b}^{\mathrm{annealed}}(k)-F_{b}^{\mathrm{constant}}(k).
\]
For the primary analysis, channels are ranked by the standardized mean activation difference on the validation set. The prespecified contrast is
\[
B_b=\frac{\Delta_b(4)+\Delta_b(8)}{2}
-\frac{\Delta_b(1)+\Delta_b(2)}{2}.
\]
A positive $B$ indicates that the annealed-minus-constant difference is larger at $k=4,8$ than at $k=1,2$; it does not require the differences at $k=4$ or $k=8$ themselves to be positive. The primary test is a two-sided one-sample Student $t$ test of $E[B]=0$ over the 20 paired replicates. The prespecified confirmation criterion requires all planned replicates, a positive mean $B$, a two-sided 95\% $t$ interval entirely above zero, and $p<0.05$. The four individual $\Delta(k)$ values are reported regardless of sign. The two alternative channel-selection rules, TwoLayerCNN, patched-run accuracy, random-channel $U$, and energy-matched $U$ are all prespecified secondary analyses.

\subsection{Stage E: secondary sensitivity analysis}\label{sec:stage-e-method}

Stage E uses 50 new paired replicates and crosses both tasks, both architectures, and six template-regularization schedules: initialization only (\texttt{template\_init}), constant $\lambda=0.1$, constant $\lambda=1$, and early, default, and late annealing schedules. This yields $50\times2\times2\times6=1{,}200$ trained models. At epoch 200, the selected-channel patching score is measured for every $k=1,\ldots,16$ under all three channel-selection rules. Energy-matched controls remain restricted to $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$.

For each non-reference schedule $p$, Stage E computes the same contrast $B$ relative to constant $\lambda=1$ regularization. Five schedule-versus-reference tests are Holm-adjusted within each task/architecture/channel-selection family. These are secondary sensitivity analyses and do not change the single Stage-D primary analysis. Because the schedules change both the timing and duration of regularization, they are treated as sensitivity analyses rather than as a single ordered regularization factor.

\subsection{Reproducibility checks}\label{sec:audit}

All Stage-D and Stage-E runs retain source hashes, protocol hashes, run manifests, and full-channel/no-op patching sanity checks. In addition, a separate implementation independently recomputed the main metrics for 32 fixed Stage-B checkpoints and read the stored scalar results only after recomputation. After correction of an error in the independent implementation described in Appendix~\ref{app:audit}, the recomputed values agreed with the stored results within every prespecified tolerance: maximum discrepancies were $2.22\times10^{-16}$ for filter-template similarity, $2.39\times10^{-7}$ for the selected-channel patching score, $1.14\times10^{-7}$ for the random-channel score, $2.19\times10^{-7}$ for $U$, and zero for ordinary accuracy and patched-run accuracy on counterfactual targets. Full-channel and no-op probability errors were zero. This check applies to the recomputed checkpoints and is not an external replication.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Constant template regularization increases filter-template similarity without a Holm-corrected patching advantage}\label{sec:stage-a-results}

Stage A increases filter-template similarity in all four constant-regularization-versus-initialization-only comparisons, but none of the four selected-minus-random $U$ tests survives Holm correction. For example, on \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, constant regularization increases filter-template similarity by 0.289 but reduces test accuracy by 6.13 percentage points at 40 epochs; the paired $\Delta U=0.0252$ has marginal 95\% interval $[-0.0306,0.0811]$ and Holm-adjusted $p=0.9995$. On \texttt{single\_shape}/TinyCNN the marginal $\Delta U$ interval is positive, but its adjusted $p=0.1633$. Stage A therefore shows a strong effect on filter-template similarity without a Holm-corrected improvement in the selected-minus-random patching score.

A linear-probe analysis on frozen first-layer features shows that poor short-horizon accuracy need not imply that the features lack task-relevant information. With validation-selected alternative linear heads, mean TinyCNN test accuracy rises from 83.40\% to 99.77\% on \texttt{single\_shape} and from 52.30\% to 81.80\% on \texttt{two\_concepts}. This points to downstream readout and optimization as important contributors to the early accuracy gap.

\subsection{Annealing template regularization improves two-concept accuracy while reducing filter-template similarity}\label{sec:stage-b-results}

Stage B shows heterogeneous early effects of template initialization. Relative to unit-normalized random initialization, \texttt{template\_init} improves mean validation accuracy over epochs 1--10 by 3.34 percentage points (95\% interval $[1.25,5.42]$) on \texttt{single\_shape}/TwoLayerCNN, but is 6.84 points worse ($[-10.26,-3.43]$) on \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN. Thus, template initialization does not provide a consistent early optimization advantage across the tested settings.

Longer training removes much of the early TinyCNN accuracy gap on \texttt{two\_concepts}. The initialization-only condition rises from 97.66\% test accuracy at epoch 40 to 99.30\% at epoch 200, matching unit-normalized random initialization at the final checkpoint. Constant template regularization reaches 98.01\%, while annealed regularization reaches 99.22\%. The annealed-minus-constant accuracy difference is 1.21 percentage points (95\% interval $[0.71,1.71]$), while filter-template similarity decreases from 0.947 to 0.580. In TwoLayerCNN, the corresponding accuracy difference is 0.39 points ($[0.04,0.74]$), while filter-template similarity decreases from 0.999 to 0.907.

In this setting, constant regularization keeps the first-layer filters close to the templates, whereas annealing the penalty allows them to drift farther while slightly improving test accuracy on \texttt{two\_concepts}. The experiment does not establish that reduced filter-template similarity causes the accuracy improvement.

\subsection{The random-channel baseline changes the selected-minus-random patching score}\label{sec:decomposition}

Stage C initially showed a sign change in \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN: the annealed-minus-constant difference in $U_{\mathrm{random}}$ was negative for $k=1,2$ and positive for $k=4,8$ under all three channel-selection rules. Decomposing $U$ changes the interpretation. When channels are ranked by activation difference, the exploratory differences in selected-channel patching score were $-0.375$, $-0.163$, $-0.022$, and $+0.006$ for $k=1,2,4,8$, respectively, whereas the corresponding random-channel differences were $-0.040$, $-0.062$, $-0.083$, and $-0.033$. Thus, $\Delta U(4)=+0.061$ even though the selected-channel score is slightly lower with annealed regularization, because the random-channel score decreases more strongly.

The same selected-channel pattern appears under the AUROC and validation-patching channel-selection rules: strongly negative at one or two channels, near zero or slightly negative at four, and slightly positive at eight. An energy-matched control changes the selected-minus-control score but not the selected-channel patching score itself. Matching is generally tight for $k=4$ and $k=8$ but can be poor for single-channel interventions, so the energy-matched analysis remains secondary. This decomposition motivated the use of the selected-channel patching score, rather than $U$, as the Stage-D primary outcome.

\[

95\%\ \mathrm{CI}=[0.273680,0.388273],

with $t_{19}=12.0905$ and two-sided $p=2.28\times10^{-10}$. The prespecified confirmation criterion is therefore satisfied.

\subsection{One-to-one matching confirms distinct template matches under constant regularization}\label{sec:matching-results}

The exploratory matching analysis on the 2,000 saved Stage-B checkpoint states asks whether the high filter-template similarity under constant regularization could arise because many learned filters repeatedly select the same nearest template. Maximum-weight one-to-one assignment prevents such reuse by matching each learned filter to a distinct template. At epoch 200, one-to-one similarity under constant regularization ranges from 0.9468 to 0.9999 across the four task/architecture settings, with essentially no difference from the nearest-template score, whereas the annealed-regularization means range from 0.5743 to 0.9425. The high similarity under constant regularization therefore persists under the stricter one-to-one matching rule.

\section{Interpretation}\label{sec:interpretation}

Constant template regularization strongly increases filter-template similarity, while the annealed-minus-constant difference in the normalized patching score varies with the number of patched channels. In the primary TinyCNN setting, annealed regularization gives substantially lower scores at one and two patched channels than at four and eight. TwoLayerCNN shows a different pattern. A result at a single channel count therefore does not summarize the regularization-schedule comparison across the tested models.

The distinction between the selected-channel patching score and a selected-minus-control score also matters. In Stage C, the positive annealed-minus-constant value of $U_{\mathrm{random}}(4)$ occurs even though the selected-channel score is slightly lower under annealed regularization, because the random-channel score decreases more strongly. Reporting the selected intervention and the control baseline separately avoids attributing a relative change to the selected channels alone.

These results concern the sensitivity of the patching measurement to intervention size, not the identification of a unique mechanism. Variation with the number of patched channels could reflect redundancy, nonlinear channel interactions, the channel-selection rule, activation scale, or out-of-distribution combinations of activations. The different pattern in TwoLayerCNN further indicates that the result should not be treated as a general property of template-based regularization.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item Only two related synthetic tasks, two small architectures, one first-layer template bank, one optimizer, and one family of regularization schedules are studied. There is no natural-image benchmark or human-interpretability experiment.
\item The renderer provides balanced, matched counterfactuals and exact object masks. This control is useful for measurement but simplifies correlations and nuisance structure found in real data.
\item The confirmed Stage-D result is specific to \texttt{two\_concepts}/TinyCNN, ranking channels by activation difference, epoch 200, and $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$. Stage-E evidence broadens the sensitivity analysis but remains secondary.
\item Stage E explores six regularization schedules and all $k=1,\ldots,16$. Holm adjustment is applied only within the prespecified five-comparison families; these analyses should not be used to select favorable post hoc subgroups.
\item Activation patching replaces complete first-layer feature maps and can create activation combinations that are out of distribution for the model. The normalized patching score is tied to probability outputs, the chosen layer, and the channel-selection rule; other intervention metrics may give different results \citep{zhang_2024_activation-patching,heimersheim_2024_activation-patching}.
\item $U_{\mathrm{random}}$ and $U_{\mathrm{energy}}$ are selected-minus-control scores. The random baseline changes with the number of patched channels, while energy matching is imperfect, especially for single-channel interventions. Neither score should replace the directly reported selected-channel patching score.
\item The one-to-one matching analysis concerns a redundant rank-10 template bank and does not show that every filter has a distinct semantic meaning or that the filters are human-interpretable.
\item The independent reimplementation check covers 32 fixed Stage-B checkpoints rather than every checkpoint from every stage. Agreement on these checkpoints supports the implementation check but is not an external replication.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and future work}\label{sec:future}

Future experiments could directly test channel redundancy, joint versus single-channel effects, subspace-level interventions, activation-scale controls, and architecture-by-channel-count interactions under a prespecified analysis plan. Experiments beyond the present renderer would test whether the channel-count dependence persists when concept correlations and nuisance structure are less controlled. Claims about human interpretability would require direct human evaluation rather than inference from filter-template similarity.

Natural-image experiments would address external validity, and larger architectures could help determine whether the TwoLayerCNN result reflects depth, optimization, or a property of this particular network. Alternative intervention targets and evaluation metrics would test whether the confirmed contrast is specific to first-layer patching with the present probability-based score.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Constant template regularization produces high first-layer filter-template similarity in these small CNNs. Activation patching gives a more conditional result. In the prespecified TinyCNN confirmation, the annealed-minus-constant difference in selected-channel patching score is strongly negative at one and two patched channels, close to zero at four, and slightly positive at eight. A separate 1,200-model sensitivity study gives positive values of the same prespecified channel-count contrast for TinyCNN on both tasks, while TwoLayerCNN behaves differently. The selected-minus-random analysis also shows that the control baseline can change a relative patching comparison. Filter-template similarity, selected-channel patching score, and selected-minus-control scores should therefore be reported separately, together with the number of patched channels and the architecture.
