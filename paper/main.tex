\title[Activation Patching in Structured CNNs]{Activation Patching in Structured CNNs: Intervention Size, Architecture, and Additivity}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Heriberto} \sur{Espino Montelongo}}
\affil*[1]{\orgname{Universidad de las Américas Puebla}, \city{San Andrés Cholula}, \state{Puebla}, \country{Mexico}}

\abstract{Activation patching measures how model outputs change when internal activations are replaced, but the measured effect can depend on the intervention itself. We study controlled convolutional networks whose first layer is initialized from a predefined bank of edge, corner, and ring filters. Keeping these filters near the bank produces high weight-space similarity, yet the release-versus-retention patching effect changes with the number of patched channels and with the architecture after the patched layer. A pixel-permuted control further shows that the two-dimensional arrangement of the filter bank matters beyond its Gram matrix, rank, and singular spectrum. We then decompose joint interventions into the sum of singleton channel effects plus a residual. Large intervention-size effects remain in analytically additive networks, showing that such effects do not by themselves imply channel interactions. In deeper networks, especially some BatchNorm variants, observed and additive predictions can diverge substantially. The structured-versus-pixel-permuted difference is largely preserved by the additive reconstruction. These results separate four quantities that are often conflated in mechanistic interpretation: filter similarity, singleton-effect geometry, metric nonlinearity, and genuine downstream non-additivity.}

\keywords{Explainable AI, activation patching, convolutional neural networks, mechanistic interpretability, predefined filters}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Early convolutional filters are unusually easy to inspect because their weights can still be viewed as spatial kernels. This has motivated fixed wavelet and Gabor representations, Gabor initialization, constrained Gabor layers, and networks built from predefined spatial filters \cite{bruna_2013_scattering-networks,ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization,molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor,linse_2024_predefined-filters,orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. These approaches ask whether useful first-layer structure can be specified or maintained during training.

A separate question is whether preserving that structure gives a predictable functional role. Weight similarity, representational alignment, and influence on model outputs are different properties. Concept-based methods have repeatedly shown that a representation can align with a human-defined concept without being pure, complete, or used in the expected way \cite{kim_2018_tcav,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks,margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. The same distinction applies at the filter level: a learned kernel can remain close to a predefined template without implying that interventions on the corresponding activation channel have a simple effect.

Activation patching offers a direct intervention. An activation from one input is inserted into another forward pass and the resulting output change is measured. However, patching results are sensitive to the source and target inputs, the component being replaced, the number of components replaced, and the output metric \cite{heimersheim_2024_activation-patching,zhang_2024_activation-patching,miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. A multi-channel intervention can also look nonlinear even when channel effects combine additively in logit space, because individual effects can cancel and the evaluation metric itself can be nonlinear.

We study these issues in a controlled vision setting. Every model begins with the same 16-channel first convolution initialized from a bank of edge, corner, and ring filters. In the \emph{retention} condition, an $L_2$ penalty keeps the first layer close to the bank throughout training. In the \emph{release} condition, the same penalty is annealed to zero. Matched counterfactual images make it possible to replace complete first-layer feature maps with activations from another valid image while leaving downstream parameters unchanged during the intervention.

The experiments address four questions. First, does the release-versus-retention patching difference depend on how many channels are patched? Second, does the same first-layer intervention behave differently when the network after the patched layer changes? Third, is the effect specific to the original two-dimensional filter arrangement, or can it be reproduced by a pixel permutation that preserves the bank's Gram matrix, row norms, rank, and singular spectrum? Fourth, how much of the intervention-size effect can be reconstructed from singleton channel effects, and how much requires genuine downstream non-additivity?

The main result is that these questions have different answers. Filter similarity can remain high while patching effects change substantially. Intervention size and architecture both matter. The original spatial arrangement has a measurable effect relative to the pixel-permuted control, but that effect is architecture dependent. Most importantly, dependence on intervention size is not equivalent to interaction among patched channels: exact-additivity controls retain large intervention-size contrasts. The paper therefore treats multi-channel patching as a composition of singleton geometry, metric choice, and residual downstream interaction rather than as a single mechanistic quantity.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Structured first-layer filters}

Predefined and constrained filters have been used to encode known spatial structure before or during CNN training. Wavelet scattering provides a fixed multiscale representation \cite{bruna_2013_scattering-networks}. Gabor-based work has used structured initialization, explicit maintenance of filter structure, and learnable Gabor parameterizations \cite{ozbulak_2018_gabor-initialization,molaei_2020_gabor-filter-structure,wang_2024_learnable-gabor,orozco-solis_2024_learnable-gabor-filters}. Predefined-filter networks and frozen random convolutions further show that later layers can make effective use of spatial features that are not learned freely \cite{linse_2024_predefined-filters,gaudio_2023_fixed-filters,gavrikov_2023_random-convolutions}. This literature establishes that first-layer structure can persist and remain useful, but it does not imply a unique causal role for individual activation channels.

\subsection{Explanations, interventions, and faithfulness}

Concept-based approaches such as TCAV and concept bottlenecks make internal representations easier to inspect or edit \cite{kim_2018_tcav,koh_2020_concept-bottlenecks}. Subsequent work shows that alignment and influence can diverge \cite{margeloiu_2021_concept-bottleneck-alignment,nicolson_2025_explaining-explainability,sharma_2026_encoding-without-influence}. Activation patching shifts the focus from decodability to interventions, but the resulting quantity still depends on the experimental definition. In particular, alternative corruption schemes, metrics, and ablation procedures can produce different localization or faithfulness conclusions \cite{zhang_2024_activation-patching,miller_2024_circuit-faithfulness}. Our contribution is to examine this measurement problem in small CNNs with deliberately structured first-layer filters and to add an explicit singleton-additivity baseline before interpreting intervention-size effects as interactions.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Controlled tasks and filter bank}

We use two $32\times32$ grayscale procedural classification tasks. In \texttt{single\_shape}, the four classes are line, circle, triangle, and square. In \texttt{two\_concepts}, circle and triangle presence define two binary factors and therefore four classes. Rendering varies angle, line width, scale, translation, Gaussian noise, and occasional occlusion. Counterfactual pairs change the class-defining factor while holding nuisance variables fixed.

Every model begins with a bias-free $1\!\to\!16$ convolution with $9\times9$ kernels, same padding, and ReLU. The predefined bank contains eight oriented edge filters, four two-ray corners, and four rings. Each filter is centered and normalized to unit norm. The flattened $16\times81$ bank has numerical rank 10. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the renderer, filter bank, and patching location.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/fig00_overview.pdf}
\caption{Controlled experimental system. Representative procedural inputs, the 16 predefined first-layer filters, and the activation-patching intervention. Selected post-ReLU feature maps in a base run are replaced by the corresponding feature maps from a matched counterfactual run.}
\label{fig:overview}
\end{figure}

The first-layer kernels are initialized from the predefined bank $W_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Training minimizes cross-entropy plus
\begin{equation}
\lambda(e)\,
\frac{\lVert W-W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2}
     {\lVert W_{\mathrm{ref}}\rVert_F^2}.
\label{eq:regularization}
\end{equation}
Retention keeps $\lambda=1$ throughout 200 epochs. Release keeps $\lambda=1$ through epoch 10, decays it linearly to zero by epoch 80, and then trains without the penalty. These are two complete training regimes; downstream weights are allowed to co-adapt.

\subsection{Activation patching and evaluation}

For every matched pair, the post-ReLU first-layer activations are cached. Channels are ranked on the validation set by the absolute standardized difference in their global-max activations between the two counterfactual states. Test data are not used for ranking. The top $k$ channels are patched for $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$; random same-size channel sets are used as controls.

We report two principal output measures. Centered logits remove the common-logit offset,
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\ell}
=
\ell-\mathrm{mean}(\ell)\mathbf 1,
\label{eq:centered-logits}
\end{equation}
and centered-logit fidelity measures how much patching moves the base output toward the counterfactual output in this space. We also use the unnormalized reduction in squared probability error,
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{prob}}(S)
=
E_{\mathrm{prob}}(0,1)-E_{\mathrm{prob}}(S,1),
\label{eq:prob-reduction}
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the patched channel set. Using both avoids relying on a single normalization convention.

For any patching metric $M$, let $\Delta^M(k)$ be release minus retention at intervention size $k$. The intervention-size contrast is
\begin{equation}
B^M
=
\frac{\Delta^M(4)+\Delta^M(8)}{2}
-
\frac{\Delta^M(1)+\Delta^M(2)}{2}.
\label{eq:B}
\end{equation}
Positive $B$ means that the release-minus-retention difference is larger on average at $k=4,8$ than at $k=1,2$.

The spatial control applies one common random permutation of the 81 spatial coordinates to every filter in the bank. This destroys the original edge/corner/ring arrangement while preserving the complete Gram matrix, row norms, numerical rank, singular spectrum, and each row's coefficient multiset.

\subsection{Singleton-additive reconstruction}

To separate intervention-size dependence from multi-channel interaction, we re-evaluate the saved architecture and spatial-control checkpoints one channel at a time. Let $\ell_0$ denote the base logits, $\ell_{\{j\}}$ the logits after patching channel $j$ alone, and $\ell_S$ the logits after jointly patching the set $S$. Define
\begin{equation}
d_j=\ell_{\{j\}}-\ell_0,\qquad
\widehat{\ell}_S=\ell_0+\sum_{j\in S}d_j,\qquad
r_S=\ell_S-\widehat{\ell}_S.
\label{eq:additive}
\end{equation}
The same output metrics are computed from $\ell_S$ and from the additive reconstruction $\widehat{\ell}_S$. Thus, a curved metric profile is not automatically counted as model non-additivity. We also measure cancellation among singleton effects using
\begin{equation}
K(S)
=
1-
\frac{\left\|\sum_{j\in S}d_j\right\|_2}
     {\sum_{j\in S}\|d_j\|_2}.
\label{eq:cancellation}
\end{equation}
The tiny architectures provide an analytic control because their downstream computation is pooling followed by a linear classifier; whole-channel patching is therefore additive in logits up to numerical precision.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Filter similarity does not determine patching behavior}

Retention preserves the intended first-layer structure. On \texttt{two\_concepts}, mean template similarity is 0.944 under retention and 0.574 under release in TinyCNN, while test accuracy remains high in both conditions (98.05\% and 99.45\%). In the corresponding two-layer network, template similarities are 0.999 and 0.896 with accuracies of 99.11\% and 99.26\%. Thus the regularizer strongly changes final filter geometry without producing a comparable separation in ordinary predictive accuracy.

The functional intervention is less simple. In the held-out 20-block TinyCNN test, the original patching contrast is positive,
$B=0.33098$ with a 95\% confidence interval of $[0.27368,0.38827]$. The release-minus-retention differences at $k=1,2,4,8$ are $-0.3459$, $-0.3317$, $-0.0221$, and $+0.0065$, respectively. A single intervention size would therefore give an incomplete description of the comparison.

The same contrast remains positive under the two principal alternative metrics: $B=+0.17888$ for centered-logit fidelity and $B=+0.89441$ for probability-error reduction. Their pointwise curves differ, however, confirming that intervention size and evaluation metric define different views of the same intervention.

\subsection{Downstream architecture strongly changes the effect}

Changing only the network after the patched first layer produces large differences in $B$. Across 16 architectures and 100 independent renderer blocks, the repeated-measures architecture effect is
$F(15,1485)=77.09$ with partial $\eta^2=0.438$ for centered-logit fidelity and
$F(15,1485)=656.70$ with partial $\eta^2=0.869$ for probability-error reduction. The result remains under Greenhouse--Geisser correction, within-block permutation tests, Friedman tests, and random channel orders.

Figure~\ref{fig:main-results} shows the central pattern. Under global-max pooling, most of the Tiny-to-plain change appears when the first downstream convolution is added; adding more plain depth changes less. Under global-average pooling the pattern differs. Residual connections produce comparatively small changes, while BatchNorm and width are strongly context dependent. The result is therefore architecture dependence rather than a universal effect of one architectural ingredient.

\subsection{The spatial arrangement of the filter bank matters}

The pixel-permuted experiment asks whether the effect can be attributed only to retaining a fixed low-rank bank. It cannot. On \texttt{two\_concepts}, the structured-minus-pixel-permuted difference in selected-channel centered-logit $B$, averaged across four architectures, is $+0.05743$ with a 95\% interval of $[+0.05143,+0.06361]$. For probability-error reduction the corresponding difference is $+0.04563$ with interval $[+0.03715,+0.05374]$.

The effect is not uniform across architectures. It is positive for \texttt{tiny\_gmp}, \texttt{tiny\_gap}, and \texttt{plain2\_w16\_gap}, but negative for \texttt{plain2\_w16\_gmp}. Random channel subsets yield substantially smaller architecture-averaged differences. Figure~\ref{fig:spatial} shows both the sign reversal and the selected-versus-random comparison.

These results isolate a role for spatial organization beyond simple rank or pairwise filter geometry, while also showing that the sign and magnitude of this role depend on how the first-layer representation is subsequently processed.

\subsection{Intervention-size effects do not imply channel interactions}

The singleton reconstruction changes the interpretation of the earlier $k$-dependence result. In the analytically additive tiny networks, observed and reconstructed curves agree to numerical precision even when $B$ is large. On \texttt{two\_concepts}/\texttt{tiny\_gmp}, centered-logit fidelity gives $B=+0.14558$ for both observed and additive curves; probability-error reduction gives $B=+0.83151$ for both. The intervention-size effect therefore survives even when the joint logit change is exactly the sum of the singleton changes.

Cancellation increases with intervention size. In the structured-filter release condition on \texttt{single\_shape}, the mean cancellation score across the four spatial-control architectures is approximately 0.20 at $k=4$, 0.32 at $k=8$, and 0.45 at $k=16$. Joint effects can therefore flatten or change shape simply because singleton logit vectors point in different directions.

Deeper networks add genuine non-additivity. On \texttt{single\_shape}/\texttt{bn2\_w16\_gap}, centered-logit $B$ is $-0.27012$ for the observed curve but $+0.34955$ under the additive reconstruction, reversing sign. On \texttt{bn4\_w16\_gap}, the corresponding values are $+0.37725$ and $+1.64910$. These discrepancies require a nonzero residual $r_S$ and cannot be attributed to metric nonlinearity alone.

Finally, the structured-versus-pixel-permuted effect is already present to a large extent in the additive component. Averaged across the four spatial-control architectures on \texttt{two\_concepts}, the centered-logit difference is $+0.05743$ observed and $+0.05710$ additive. For probability-error reduction the values are $+0.04563$ and $+0.04105$. Figure~\ref{fig:additivity} summarizes both results.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The experiments separate several claims that are easy to conflate. First, keeping first-layer kernels close to recognizable spatial templates is a statement about weights. It does not determine how interventions on their activation channels affect model outputs. Second, a patching effect that changes with the number of patched channels is not, by itself, evidence for synergy or another nonlinear interaction. Exact-additivity controls show that singleton geometry and metric choice are sufficient to produce strong intervention-size dependence. Third, genuine non-additivity does occur, but it depends strongly on downstream architecture.

This distinction helps interpret the architecture study. The large differences across CNN variants are not merely different values of the same hidden mechanism. Some architectures largely preserve the additive singleton prediction, whereas others transform joint interventions enough to change the magnitude or even the sign of $B$. BatchNorm variants provide the clearest examples in the present grid. Architecture therefore determines how strongly a first-layer perturbation is recombined after the intervention point.

The pixel-permuted control adds another layer. The structured bank and its permutation have identical Gram matrices, rank, singular spectra, row norms, and coefficient multisets, but they do not produce the same patching behavior. The original two-dimensional arrangement therefore matters functionally. At the same time, the additive reconstruction reproduces most of the structured-versus-permuted difference. This suggests that the spatial bank primarily changes the individual channel contributions and how those contributions align, while architecture-specific downstream computation can further modify them.

The study also illustrates a practical lesson for activation-patching work. A mechanistic claim about interactions should be compared with a singleton-additive baseline. Otherwise, curvature caused by vector cancellation or by a nonlinear evaluation metric can be mistaken for model-level interaction. Reporting the intervention size, patched component, source/target construction, channel-selection rule, metric, and architecture makes the measured quantity easier to interpret.

The scope is deliberately controlled. The tasks are synthetic, the networks are small CNNs, and the filters come from one predefined bank. The retention-versus-release comparison also changes the complete training trajectory, so it does not isolate the effect of replacing final first-layer weights while holding the rest of a trained network fixed. The singleton decomposition is post-hoc and reuses the same checkpoints and renderer blocks as the source experiments. Its role is diagnostic: it separates superposition from residual non-additivity, but it does not uniquely identify which downstream layer causes that residual.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Preserving predefined first-layer filters does not determine a unique activation-patching effect. In controlled CNNs, the release-versus-retention comparison depends on intervention size and on the architecture after the patched layer, while a Gram- and rank-matched pixel permutation shows that the original spatial arrangement of the filter bank also matters.

The singleton decomposition provides the main mechanistic clarification. Large intervention-size effects remain in networks that are exactly additive in logits, so dependence on $k$ should not be interpreted as evidence of channel interaction without an explicit additive baseline. Deeper networks can introduce substantial residual non-additivity, and the amount of that residual is architecture dependent. Much of the structured-versus-pixel-permuted effect, however, is already present in the geometry of singleton channel contributions.

Taken together, the results argue for separating structural alignment, singleton-effect geometry, metric dependence, and downstream interaction when interpreting activation-patching experiments.
